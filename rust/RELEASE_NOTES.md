Rubylight, called Butterpollo until rc.29, is written in Rust: host, native helpers, service and setup, with a Svelte web console. The 3.0.0 installer is named `rubylight-setup-3.0.0.exe` and upgrades an existing Vibepollo or Butterpollo installation in place, keeping settings, paired devices, the app library and covers. Codec SDKs and Windows drivers remain external components; the setup installs the drivers.

## New in 3.0.0

Rubylight 3.0.0 is the stable release of the 3.0 line. It covers everything since 2.6.0, including the nine previews from 3.0.0-alpha.1 to rc.5. It brings split-screen co-op and background streams through Nucleus Co-op, a picture that holds through scene cuts and lost frames, a bitrate that follows the network, Dolby Vision, HDR10+ and HLG, sound for receivers and soundbars, Neural Rendering, and a simpler console. Stock Moonlight clients keep working and get the streaming fixes too. Formats and features that need the client's support go only to a client that asks for them, and the protocol version is unchanged. Nightjar 4.0 is the matching client.

### Highlights

- **The picture holds through loss.** Scene cuts no longer freeze the stream (15 freezes to 0 in our test), and H.264 and HEVC repair a lost frame with a small frame instead of a keyframe.
- **The bitrate follows the network.** When the link can't carry the stream, the host lowers the bitrate and comes back by itself, PyroWave included.
- **Play together.** Split-screen co-op gives each player their own copy of the game, and background streams play away from your monitors, both through Nucleus Co-op.
- **Neural Rendering.** A switch on a game's page installs a neural upscaling mod into games with DLSS, FSR or XeSS, and takes it out again.
- **Dolby Vision, HDR10+ and HLG.** HDR streams carry per-scene brightness data, Dolby Vision works on HEVC and AV1, and screens that show only HLG get HLG.
- **Sound for your receiver.** PCM up to 7.1.4, Dolby Digital and Dolby Digital Plus, plus 7.1.4 surround, night mode, headphone surround and haptics from game audio.
- **A simpler console.** Home walks you through pairing and playing, Games lists every launcher, Settings shows the ten settings most people change, and pairing takes a QR code.
- **Clipboard, files and USB.** Copied text and images go both ways, shared folders and file transfer reach the PC's files, and USB devices can be forwarded to the PC.

### Upgrading from 2.6.x

Rubylight 3.0 needs an AMD Radeon GPU with a working AMF encoder, on Windows 10 or 11; NVIDIA and Intel graphics stay on [Rubylight 2.6.0](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/releases/tag/v2.6.0). Setup upgrades 2.6.x, Vibepollo and Butterpollo in place and keeps your settings, paired devices, games and covers.

### Picture and encoding

- **Scene cuts no longer freeze the picture.** When a scene cut comes out of the AMD encoder too large to keep its error correction, the host encodes it again as a keyframe that fits. In our 250 Mbps HEVC 1440p60 test at 0.3 % random loss, lost scene cuts went from 15–16 to 0, with no change in encode time. [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md#october-28-lost-scene-cuts-and-recovery-keyframes-past-four-fec-blocks).
- **Recovery keyframes keep their error correction.** The keyframe that repairs a lost frame is kept small enough for its full error correction, so random loss no longer takes it too. In the same test, freezes over 100 ms went from 4 to 0 and picture age dropped by 3 ms. When keyframes still come out more than a tenth over the automatic frame cap, the host lowers that cap until they fit.
- **Faster repair after a lost frame on H.264 and HEVC**, for every client. Instead of a full keyframe, the host sends a frame predicted from an older picture the device still has. At 1440p120 that frame was 3 % to 46 % smaller than the keyframe when 1 or 2 frames were lost; for longer losses the host still sends the keyframe. `amd_ltr_frames = 0` turns it off.
- **AV1 repairs without intra refresh.** With intra refresh, the AMD driver turned off the references a repair needs. Now a loss costs a 48 KB repair frame instead of a 101 KB keyframe, and ordinary frames are about a third smaller (33.5 KB instead of 51.2 KB at 1440p120 and 50 Mbps). [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md#october-27-av1-intra-refresh-and-recovery-after-a-lost-frame).
- **Fresher frames with VRR.** The host now times the game's own frames instead of each capture, and waits for the game's next frame when it is close. A 120 Hz game in a 60 fps VRR stream reaches the encoder 0.27 ms old instead of 4.7 ms (median) and leaves the host 3.5 ms sooner. [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md#october-26-vrr-claims-follow-the-games-pictures).
- **A focused VRR game keeps its own pace beside another window.** On Windows 11 24H2 and newer, another window's animation or a pointer move no longer makes a focused game repeat and skip frames. With a 60 Hz game at 60 fps on a 240 Hz VRR desktop and another window animating beside it, repeated game frames fell from 159–294 to 0–20 per minute, and capture age p99 from 6.2–16.5 ms to 0.4–6.1 ms. A game alone on the display is paced as before. [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md#october-30-a-focused-games-pictures-beside-another-window).
- **Three stream presets.** **Stream preset** under Streaming picks Lowest latency, Balanced (the default, which changes nothing) or Reference quality, for the host, a device or an app. A value you set yourself always wins. Lowest latency made the first keyframe 29 % smaller on HEVC and 38–40 % smaller on H.264, with latency within 0.15 ms of the others. [Stream presets](../docs/configuration.md#stream-presets).
- **Full chroma takes effect when you save it**, with no host restart. Turning it off, or turning off HEVC or AV1, withdraws it at once. `/serverinfo` lists the packed 4:4:4 modes the host offers (`Packed444Modes`), and a slow first frame no longer gets packed 4:4:4 refused.
- **10-bit SDR on request.** A client that asks for a 10-bit format with `hdrMode=0` gets a 10-bit SDR stream, packed 4:4:4 included.
- **PyroWave is there when you need it.** It recovers when the GPU device it converts on is lost, the host checks again a minute later when its first check timed out, and a host started without administrator rights offers it again (its check took 24 s there instead of 1.4 s).
- **Slice delivery** (`video_slice_delivery = 4`, off by default). The host starts sending an H.264 or HEVC frame while the rest of it is still encoding. At 4K60 HEVC and 80 Mbps the picture arrived 1.3 to 1.5 ms sooner on loopback and 1.2 to 1.4 ms sooner over Wi-Fi 6E. At 5 % loss, 8 frames could not be rebuilt with it on and none with it off, so it stays off by default. [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md#october-23-slice-delivery-b2-step-2f).
- **Encoder focus** (`encoder_focus`, off by default). `static` gives still parts of the picture, such as a HUD or text, more of the bitrate; `centre` also favours the middle of the screen. A codec that fails the startup check streams as before.
- **A resized game window streams at its new size**, also when the game stopped drawing after the resize.
- **Where frames go missing.** The console's stream details, the 5 s `stream timings` in the host log and Nightjar's overlay show how many frames per second the display produced and the host captured, encoded, sent and repeated.
- **Missing codecs are explained.** When a codec is missing, the host log, the console and a refused launch say why: a setting, the driver's error by name, or a video engine without that encoder. Nothing is switched off by GPU name.

### Network and bitrate

- **The bitrate steps down when the link can't carry it** (`link_rate_adaptation`, on by default). When the device loses frames that error correction can't repair, the host cuts the bitrate to what the link delivered, probes back up in small steps and returns to the requested rate after ten clean seconds. On a 50 Mbit/s link asked for 75 Mbps it cut within 1.6 s and lost 1.1 to 1.2 % of frames after the start, instead of 6.7 %. A link that carries the stream sees no change, and the stream card says "Bitrate limited by the network". [Details](../docs/configuration.md#lower-bitrate-when-the-link-cannot-carry-it).
- **PyroWave's bitrate follows the link too.** Through a link limited to 250 Mbit/s, a 400 Mbps PyroWave stream used to lose 32 % of its packets with no picture arriving whole; now it settles at 250 to 290 Mbps, loses 2.7 % and gets 81 % of pictures through whole. It stops at PyroWave's lowest workable bitrate (187 Mbps at 1080p60), and below that the warning says to use HEVC or AV1.
- **Error correction the device chooses.** A device can pick its own share of recovery packets, or have the host follow its once-a-second loss reports. On a shaped link modelling 60 Mbit/s Wi-Fi, following the device cut median loss from 2.37 to 0.26 % and freezes from 21 to 1. The PC's own setting stays the default, and `fec_max_percentage` (50 % by default) caps both. [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md#october-27-client-error-correction-and-a-link-test-before-the-first-bitrate-nuc).
- **No slow pace by mistake.** A clean stream no longer falls to the slow pace for the session, or for the next day, because a decoder asked twice for a keyframe ([#59](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/59)), the PC's own network adapter refused packets as a game started, Alt+Tab or a bitrate change sent a keyframe ([#48](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/48)), or the first keyframe was lost. A device asking again for the same repair no longer lowers the bitrate either. Pacing history saved by older versions is dropped once.
- **Wi-Fi pacing from the first frame.** A device that reports a Wi-Fi or mobile route (Nightjar does) gets adaptive packet pacing from the start. On a 2.4 GHz laptop link it cut unrecoverable frames from 61 to 15.7 per 90 s run, with no change on clean 5 GHz. A device that reports its wired link speed is paced for the slower port. [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md#october-25-wi-fi-pacing-hint-on-a-real-laptop).
- **Bitrate and frame rate change mid-stream**, without reconnecting and without a keyframe, for a device that asks at launch.
- **Keep streaming when the network changes.** A phone moving from Wi-Fi to mobile data keeps its stream after proving its new address with the stream key (`client_roaming`, on by default).
- **Control messages arrive right after an outage.** What the host sent during a short outage now reaches the device 85 ms after it is heard from again, instead of 1.0 to 2.9 s later. Every Moonlight client benefits.
- **Replies come from the address the device reached** ([#54](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/54)), which fixes reconnects failing with error -1 when Tailscale runs beside the LAN.
- **Video packets are the size the device asks for** ([#52](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/52)). A Moonlight asking for small packets on a remote route got a green, broken picture; the `packetsize` setting is gone.
- **Bandwidth test.** A paired device can time a short download before it picks a starting bitrate (`bandwidth_probe`, on by default; never while a stream runs).
- **Each launch starts one stream.** A second start of the same launch is refused, a stalled RTSP handshake closes after 10 s, and a launch nobody connects to expires after 30 s.

### HDR and Dolby Vision

- **HDR scene data.** HDR streams carry HDR10+ in HEVC and AV1, and HDR Vivid in HEVC, measured on the GPU for each frame (about 60 to 90 µs of CPU per frame), so a display that tone-maps with it follows each scene. Devices without HDR10+ ignore it. `hdr_dynamic_metadata` turns it off. [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md#october-27-hdr10-and-hdr-vivid-dynamic-metadata).
- **Dolby Vision on HEVC and AV1.** Devices that ask get Dolby Vision profile 8.1 on HEVC and 10.1 on AV1, over the stream's HDR10 picture, so everyone else gets HDR10 as before. On a TCL C6K TV, a Dolby Vision AV1 stream carried its data on all 2317 frames and showed as Dolby Vision from the first frame. `hdr_dolby_vision` turns it off.
- **HLG.** HEVC, AV1 and PyroWave HDR streams offer HLG to screens that show HLG and not HDR10, graded for a 1000-nit display. With HLG, HEVC also offers Dolby Vision profile 8.4. `hdr_hlg` turns it off.
- **Windows HDR Calibration on virtual displays** (`virtual_display_hdr_calibration`, off by default). A new virtual display gets the calibration profile of the PC's physical HDR display for the stream.
- 10-bit SDR streams never carry HDR10+, HDR Vivid or Dolby Vision data.

### Audio

- **PCM, Dolby Digital and Dolby Digital Plus.** A device that asks gets uncompressed PCM (stereo up to 7.1.4), or Dolby Digital and Dolby Digital Plus (stereo and 5.1), for a TV, soundbar or receiver to decode. Dolby comes in 32 ms frames, so it reaches the network up to about 30 ms later than Opus, which stays the default. Encoding Dolby takes about a fifth of the CPU Opus takes. [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md#october-27-audio-passthrough-encode-cost).
- **7.1.4 surround.** A device that asks for 12 channels, such as Nightjar on Android or Windows, or Moonlight V+, gets 7.1.4 Opus. On 7.1, 5.1 or stereo speakers the height channels are mixed into the speakers below.
- **Night mode** (`audio_night_mode`) evens out loud explosions and quiet dialogue, with no added buffering.
- **Headphone surround** (`audio_headphone_surround`) renders the game's 7.1 or 5.1 mix for headphones on a stereo device. The stream stays stereo Opus, so every Moonlight client works. Both are off by default and can be set for the host, a device, an app or one session.
- **Switch them from the device.** A device can turn Night mode and Headphone surround on or off during its own stream, and the change carries to its next session of the app. Each streaming device changes only its own stream. Nightjar's stream menu has both switches.
- **Haptics from game audio** (`audio_haptics`, off by default). A DualSense on the device feels the game's bass and impacts when the game sends no vibration of its own.
- **No audio gap when the game goes quiet.** The host keeps sending at its normal pace through silence, so devices no longer hear an underrun each time.

### Play together

- **Split-screen co-op.** A game Nucleus Co-op supports gets a second entry with "(Co-op)" after its name. Each device that launches it takes the next free slot and streams its own copy of the game, with its own controller, picture and sound. **Play together** › **Set up co-op** on the game's page downloads Nucleus Co-op and gets the game ready. [Co-op](../docs/nucleus-coop.md).
- **Each player gets only their own game.** A stream shows only that player's window, plays only its sound (Windows build 20348 or newer) and sends rumble back to that device. Keyboard, mouse and a second controller never reach the PC's desktop.
- **Co-op on Home.** Home shows each running co-op game with its players. **Remove** takes a player out, **End co-op** ends every stream, and **Let … join** lets another paired device in. A device that drops keeps its slot for two minutes, and **Can join co-op games** on a device's page keeps one out.
- **Background streams through Nucleus Co-op.** A background stream runs the game as its own instance, away from your monitors, so the PC's displays, layout and focus stay with you. Background streams take controllers only, and get a card on Home with **End stream**. A saved `background_mode` becomes `nucleus`.
- **One-player games stream in the background too**, without a handler from Nucleus Co-op's hub ([#59](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/59)): Rubylight writes its own handler and adds the game to Nucleus Co-op's list. When the game needs no administrator rights, it runs as you, from your own copy of Nucleus Co-op.
- **Steam games start as Steam would start them**, with their launch options and Steam app id, so they don't restart through Steam. A launcher's own .exe, or a frontend's, is refused with the reason.
- **What can't stream says why first.** A game with anti-cheat, including Valve Anti-Cheat and Battle.net's Blizzard games, a game on a network share and a program in the Windows folder are refused before anything is written. The Play together panel says what will happen before you launch, also on an account without administrator rights.
- **Nucleus Co-op stays out of your way.** **Close Nucleus Co-op** sits next to **Open Nucleus Co-op on this PC**, Set up co-op no longer hangs on its loading screen, and a background stream no longer waits for a question nobody at the PC answers. When Nucleus Co-op still doesn't start the game, the stream says why.
- **Your compatibility settings come back.** Nucleus Co-op changes a game's Windows compatibility settings and never puts them back; Rubylight now does when the session ends, also after a crash.
- Rubylight downloads Nucleus Co-op 2.4.2 at the first co-op launch, checks its size and checksum, and installs it where only administrators can change it. **Nucleus path** points at your own copy instead. Co-op needs the host to run as the Windows service.

### Games and library

- **Neural Rendering.** A switch on a game's page installs a third-party neural upscaling mod into the game's folder, for games with DLSS, FSR or XeSS, and removes it again. The first time, a notice explains it is at your own risk, with Cancel as the default. Rubylight downloads one pinned release (0.3.5.8R2, about 1 GB, 88 s on our PC) and checks it against its pinned and published checksums. Games with anti-cheat, Valve Anti-Cheat or another upscaler mod are refused. Turning it off, or deleting or uninstalling the game, removes only what Rubylight put there. It needs Radeon graphics with RDNA 3 or RDNA 4 and 12 or more compute units, and AMD Software 26.9.1 or newer; elsewhere the switch says why it is off.
- **One game, different settings per device.** The app editor's **Per device** panel gives a game its own stream preset, frame limit, Radeon preset and game files on one device, such as 60 fps on the TV and 120 on a handheld.
- **Managed game files.** A game's own `.ini` keys, such as a frame limit, can follow the stream: a fixed value or `{stream_fps}`. The host sets them when a stream starts the game and puts them back when the session ends, also after a crash, leaving every other byte as it was. It writes only existing `.ini` files inside the game's folders, as the signed-in user.
- **Every installed game, from every launcher.** Steam, Playnite, Epic Games, Battle.net, EA app, Ubisoft Connect and GOG games are added by default, Microsoft Store and Xbox PC games are listed in Add games, and **Game folders** lists the games in folders such as `D:\Games`. A game a launcher no longer has shows "Not installed", with Remove beside it.
- **The library sync never changes an app you made.** Syncs change only the apps they added. Editing a synced game makes it yours, and a game you delete stays deleted.
- **Radeon settings per game.** A game's Radeon section has presets (Competitive, Smoothness, Quality, Custom) and plain controls for Anti-Lag, frame rate cap, Chill, sharpening and more. The host applies them when the game starts and puts them back after the session, also after a crash.
- **A confirmed game switch.** Starting another game closes the running one only when the person on the device confirms.
- **The session says when a launcher is in the way**, such as a Steam, Epic Games, EA app or Ubisoft Connect window that wants an update or a sign-in.

### Displays

- **A VRR device and a fixed-rate device share one virtual display.** A device that needs a faster refresh raises the shared display's refresh in place, so the VRR device keeps VRR.
- **A device can set its virtual display's scale.** Nightjar's **PC display scaling** sets the text and interface size on the device's virtual display without changing its resolution. Stock Moonlight is unchanged.
- **Your display choices stick.** A Windows scale you pick on the stream's virtual display comes back with the next stream ([#36](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/36)), and a display layout you set for a device is never replaced by the one a stream left behind ([#40](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/40)).
- **Rotate the display.** A device's virtual display takes its portrait or landscape shape, a monitor turns to it and back, and with Match the device the display follows a device that turns.
- **Keep monitors off while paused** (off by default). A monitor that wakes over HDMI while a device is away no longer takes the game ([#56](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/56)).
- **Virtual displays Windows moves are followed.** A virtual display Windows lists under another adapter or target, as on a Radeon 780M laptop or an RX 9070 XT with a TV ([#56](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/56)), now comes up and keeps its layout and HDR.
- **The GPU fence ring is the default** for virtual-display capture. The keyed-mutex ring stays with `idd_push_fence_ring = false`, and older display drivers keep it.
- The virtual display needs Windows 11 23H2 or later, so it is off by default on Windows 11 21H2 and 22H2, as on Windows 10. Physical displays, Windows Graphics Capture and Desktop Duplication keep working everywhere.

### Devices and input

- **The keyboard follows text fields.** When a text field on the PC gets the focus, Nightjar opens its keyboard, with digits for number fields and no suggestions for passwords (`keyboard_follows_focus`).
- **Host menu.** Hold L3+R3 for a second, press Ctrl+Alt+Backspace or hold the top-left corner for a menu drawn into the stream, so any Moonlight client can use it: Back to game, Disconnect, End session and Close game.
- **Keyboard layout.** Nightjar reports its layout, and with `always_send_scancodes` the host types the keys on your keyboard without changing the PC's input languages. `keyboard_layout` sets one for stock Moonlight.
- **Device-drawn cursor.** With Desktop Duplication capture, a device that asks draws the pointer itself and the host leaves it out of the video.
- **Touch and pen.** Per device, `touch_mode` turns touch into a mouse or a trackpad, with two-finger scroll and right click. A pen's barrel roll reaches Windows.
- **Sleep PC.** A device you allow (Sleep PC under its permissions) can put the PC to sleep while nothing streams. PCs with only Modern Standby don't offer it, and the console says why.
- **Controllers.** No more "Controller release failed" warning at every start, two Steam Deck controllers no longer get mixed up, a Nintendo controller on an older driver plays as a DualSense with its gyro, and rumble stops reach the device even with rumble off.

### Clipboard, files and USB

- **Clipboard sync.** Text copied on the device goes on the PC's clipboard and back, as you copy it, with Nightjar and Moonlight V+. Images go both ways as PNG, within size limits. The device needs the clipboard permissions.
- **Shared folders.** `shared_folders` names folders that paired devices can browse, download from and, with a write grant, upload to. Files are read and written as the signed-in user, only inside each folder.
- **File transfer.** Paired devices with Upload files or Download files can send files to the PC's Downloads\Rubylight, or a folder you choose, and get them back. It is off until you turn it on.
- **USB forwarding** (off by default). A USB device plugged into the device, such as a flash drive or a gamepad, attaches to the PC during a stream, inside a TLS tunnel on the stream's HTTPS port. No USB/IP port is open to the network. [Setup and limits](../docs/usb-forwarding.md).

### Console and pairing

- **Home, Games and a shorter menu.** Home walks a new user through three steps: pair, see your games, play. Games puts every launcher in one list with one Sync now. The sidebar keeps Home, Games, Devices, Settings and Help.
- **Settings shows what most people change.** Ten settings in plain words, and search finds every other one in All settings.
- **Help is "Something is wrong? Start here."** It reads the host log, explains known failures such as AMD encoder hangs, lost capture, ports in use or an old display driver, and says what to do. Nothing is sent anywhere.
- **Pair with a QR code.** Pairing opens with a QR code that works once, for three minutes. Nightjar refuses to pair when the PC's certificate isn't the one the code names, and a used or expired code says to show a new one.
- **A second device can play.** When you pair a device you choose Play games (the default), Watch only or Everything; before, every device after the first could only watch.
- **Wake and sleep.** Settings has a Power section for Wake-on-LAN, adapter wake settings, Sleep and Fast Startup. **Sleep after idle** (off by default) sleeps the PC only when nothing used it for that long.
- **Webhooks.** Signed events to your own URLs when devices pair, apps start and stop and streams begin and end, with a Send test button. [Webhooks](../docs/webhooks.md).
- **Client build warnings.** The device list shows each device's client build and warns when that build has a known problem with this host.

### Browsers and TVs

- **WebRTC server** (`webrtc_server`, off by default). Samsung Tizen TVs and browsers that speak the Moonlight WebRTC TV protocol can pair with a PIN and stream H.264, HEVC or AV1, with HDR, up to 7.1 audio, gamepads, keyboard and mouse, on the local network. On the same PC over loopback, H.264 1080p60 took 21 ms glass to glass (median). [Setup](../docs/webrtc.md).
- **Stream in the console.** With the WebRTC server on, the console's Stream page plays a game in the browser tab at H.264 1080p60, signed in with the console session.

### For client authors

- **Native API v1.** A paired client on the HTTPS port can read the host's capabilities, the game library with covers and the session state, and Disconnect, End session or Close game. Each device needs the Host control permission, which is off until you turn it on. [Native API](../docs/native-api.md), [control messages](../docs/control-messages.md).
- **Radeon settings from the couch.** A device with Host control can change the Radeon settings of the running session; every change is journaled and put back when the session ends, also after a crash.
- **Per-frame timing record.** `BUTTERPOLLO_TIMING_RECORD=<folder>` writes each frame's capture, convert, encode, packetize and send times. [Timing record](../docs/timing-record.md).

### Install, updates and service

- **Setup checks for an AMD GPU** and says in plain words when the PC has none or its driver has no AMF encoder.
- **Updates keep the service's startup type.** Manual, Disabled and delayed start stay as you set them.
- **Automatic update checks run on time**, also right after the PC wakes, and a failed check is retried within two minutes.
- **The display driver is repaired while the service runs**, never during a stream and at most once a day. `butterpollo-setup.exe --repair-drivers --now` repairs it at once.
- **Setup installs when an administrator owns `C:\`** ([#61](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/61)), and a refused owner is named.
- **Setup on a busy PC** no longer takes about 11 minutes for its USB/IP checks.
- Running setup again after a failed first install imports Vibepollo's or Apollo's settings. Uninstall says what it could not remove and no longer leaves 2.6.x's Intel library folder behind.

### Fixes

### Background streams and co-op

- **One-player games without a hub handler.** A one-player game streams in the background without a handler from Nucleus Co-op's hub, such as Assassin's Creed IV ([#59](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/59)): Rubylight writes its own handler and adds the game to Nucleus Co-op's list. The game runs from its own folder, and nothing is written there. A game without a hub handler says that background streams work and co-op needs a handler, and a game with a hub handler that you haven't added in Nucleus Co-op yet says to add it.
- **Your compatibility settings come back.** Nucleus Co-op sets a game's .exe to its DPI mode in your Windows compatibility settings and never puts them back. Rubylight now puts back what you had when the session ends, also after a crash or a failed start, for hub handlers too. It only touches the game's own .exe and Nucleus Co-op's instance links, and leaves a setting you changed meanwhile alone. After a host crash or a restart, they come back when you sign in at the PC. A standard Windows account can no longer block background launches by holding the lock these settings use.
- **Close Nucleus Co-op** sits next to **Open Nucleus Co-op on this PC**, and a co-op or background stream closes the Nucleus Co-op the console opened before it starts; one that won't close (it is asking something) refuses the stream until you answer it at the PC. WebView2 runs its browser without administrator rights even for an elevated Nucleus Co-op, so the handler hub view now keeps its data in the signed-in user's `AppData\Local\Butterpollo\nucleus-webview`. When Windows gives the account signed in on the PC no administrator rights to start Nucleus Co-op with (a standard account, or Windows' Administrator protection), **Set up co-op**, Open and launches say so.

A game's co-op status follows what the library lists, so a second app with the same name shows that its co-op entry is taken. The co-op panels no longer keep the PC awake or poll in a hidden tab.

### Streaming quality

- **No more slow pacing from a decoder that asks twice.** Moonlight, Artemis and Nightjar report every frame they lose, so a second keyframe request from them is their decoder asking again, not network loss. It no longer slows the stream for the rest of the session or for the next day. Clients that don't report their own losses keep the old rule.
- **A stalled network adapter on the host no longer slows the stream for good.** When only the host saw the loss (its own network adapter refused packets, as when a game starts), the slower pace lifts after 10 clean seconds and is never remembered; a second keyframe request for a frame the adapter refused counts as the host's loss too. When such stalls hold the pace for a stream, the host log names the host's network adapter and says the device reported no loss. The pacing history 3.0.0-rc.4 saved, which also held these two cases, is dropped once after the update.
- **Keyframes held to the frame cap.** When AMF encodes keyframes more than a tenth over the automatic frame cap, the host lowers that cap (it holds every frame) until keyframes fit, never below two frames' worth, raises it again once they keep to it, and goes back to the full cap for good when a lower cap does not make keyframes smaller. On our RX 7900 XT keyframes already kept to the cap.
- The host log names each recovery keyframe's size against its frame cap target, whether it was encoded again from a scene cut, and whether it left without full error correction, and the 5 s `stream timings` lines count keyframes past the cap and past full error correction.
- **Full chroma takes effect when you save it.** Turning it on checks the encoder and offers Nightjar full-resolution color without a host restart (during a stream, once the stream ends); turning it off, or turning off HEVC or AV1, withdraws it at once. A check a stream interrupts finishes after the stream, a check that fails under game load no longer withdraws a mode already offered, and "No video encoder available" no longer stays up through a working stream.
- **PyroWave no longer goes missing after a slow start.** When the host's PyroWave check times out, as when the PC is busy while the host starts, the host checks once more a minute later, when no stream runs, instead of leaving PyroWave off until a restart.
- **A resized game window streams at its new size.** A window that stopped drawing after a resize, such as a paused or unfocused game in a co-op or background stream, kept streaming its old-size picture. Its capture now starts again at the new size.

### Virtual displays

- **A VRR device and a fixed-rate device share one virtual display.** With **One shared by all devices**, a device that asked for VRR made the display 1000 Hz, and a second device at the same size without VRR was refused with "shared virtual display already uses a different mode" (and the other way round). - When a joining device switches a paused shared virtual display back on, the paused stream resumes on it without setting up the display layout again.

The answer stays a refusal (status 400) that Nightjar shows as "show a new code", not as a network problem.

## New in 3.0.0-rc.4

3.0.0-rc.4 is a preview of everything that will be in 3.0.0. It brings split-screen co-op, Dolby Vision on HEVC and AV1, HDR scene data, sound for receivers and soundbars, clipboard sync and shared folders, a simpler console, and fixes that keep the picture moving after a scene cut or a lost frame. Stock Moonlight clients keep working as before and get the streaming fixes too; formats and features that need the client's support are offered only to a client that asks for them, and the protocol version is unchanged. The new formats are written down for client authors in [docs/control-messages.md](../docs/control-messages.md) and [docs/native-api.md](../docs/native-api.md).

### Highlights

- **Split-screen co-op.** A game that Nucleus Co-op supports gets a second entry with "(Co-op)" after its name. Each player streams their own copy of the game from one PC, with their own controller, picture and sound.
- **Dolby Vision, HDR10+ and HLG.** HDR streams carry per-scene brightness data, so a TV can follow each scene instead of one fixed peak. Dolby Vision works on HEVC (profiles 8.1 and 8.4) and now on AV1 (profile 10.1). Screens that show only HLG get HLG, on PyroWave too.
- **No more freeze after a scene cut.** A scene cut that comes out too large to protect is encoded again as a keyframe that fits. In our test, freezes after scene cuts went from 15 to 0.
- **Faster recovery after a lost frame on H.264 and HEVC.** The host repairs a short loss with a frame built on an older picture the device still has, as AV1 already did, instead of a full keyframe. On for every client.
- **Sound for your receiver.** Devices can ask for PCM (up to 7.1.4) or Dolby Digital and Dolby Digital Plus, so a TV, soundbar or receiver decodes the sound itself. 7.1.4 surround, a night mode, headphone surround and haptics from game audio are new too.
- **Clipboard and folders.** Copied text goes between the device and the PC as you copy it, and images can be copied both ways. Folders you share can be browsed, downloaded from and uploaded to.
- **A simpler console.** Home walks you through pairing and playing, Games lists every launcher in one place, Settings shows the ten settings most people change, and search finds every other one. Pairing a second device lets it play right away.
- **The device's keyboard follows text fields.** Tap into a text field on the PC and Nightjar opens its keyboard, with digits for number fields and no suggestions for passwords.

### Streaming quality

- **Scene cuts no longer freeze the picture.** When a scene cut comes out of the AMD encoder too large to keep its error correction (FEC), the host encodes that picture again as a keyframe, which fits, and sends that instead. In our 250 Mbps HEVC 1440p60 test with 0.3 % random loss, lost scene cuts went from 15–16 to 0 and the short freeze after each one (about 50 ms, 15 in 45 s) is gone, with no change in encode time.
- **Recovery keyframes keep their error correction.** After a frame is lost, the keyframe that repairs it is now kept small enough to carry its full error correction within Moonlight's four blocks, so random loss on a fast wired link no longer loses it too. In the same test, freezes over 100 ms went from 4 to 0, the slow pace and bitrate cuts no longer set in, and picture age dropped by 3 ms. This covers a fixed FEC percentage and Nightjar's automatic FEC; without FEC the keyframe is held to the fewest packets the four blocks allow. It applies up to about 280 Mbps at 60 fps, where two frames still fit.
- **Faster recovery after a lost frame on H.264 and HEVC**, for every client. Instead of a full keyframe, the host sends a frame predicted from an older one the device still has. On our RX 7900 XT at 1440p120 that frame was 3 % to 46 % smaller than the keyframe when the device missed 1 or 2 frames; for longer losses the host still sends the keyframe, which is then the smaller frame. The host rewrites the frame headers so a decoder that skipped the lost frames can place the repair: every repair from 1 to 130 lost frames decoded to the same pictures as the full stream, in software and on D3D11VA. `amd_ltr_frames = 0` turns this off for all three codecs.
- **AV1 no longer uses intra refresh.** When a device asks for intra refresh on AV1, the host leaves it off and repairs a lost frame by invalidating it, as for a device that never asked. With refresh, the AMD driver turned off the long-term references that repair needs, so every loss cost a 101 KB keyframe instead of a 48 KB repair frame, and ordinary frames were half again as large. H.264 and HEVC intra refresh are unchanged.
- **Error correction the device chooses.** A device that supports it can pick its own share of recovery packets, or let the host follow its once-a-second loss reports: the host keeps the last ten seconds and sends the smallest share that keeps a lost frame under 0.1 %. **Most error correction a device may ask for** (`fec_max_percentage`, 50 % by default) caps both. For such a device the recovery packets come out of the video bitrate instead of on top of it. Nightjar on Android and desktop has the matching Error correction setting: leave it to the PC, follow the device's losses, or fix it at 10 to 50 %. On shaped Wi-Fi links, following the device's losses cut median loss from 2.37 to 0.26 % and freezes from 21 to 1 against the PC's adaptive error correction. The PC's setting stays the default until following the device has been measured on a phone over Wi-Fi.
- **Bitrate and frame rate change mid-stream.** A device that asks at launch can change its bitrate and frame rate without reconnecting. A new bitrate applies on the next frame, without a keyframe. The host caps both to the device's maximum and to what the encoder keeps up with, and answers every request with what it applied or why it refused.
- **Bandwidth test.** Paired devices can time a short download (64 KB to 4 MB, at most 3 seconds) before they pick a starting bitrate. The host refuses it while a stream runs or starts and limits how often a device may test. `bandwidth_probe` (on by default) turns it off. Nightjar's link test that uses it is off by default: on a shaped 50 Mbit/s link it held the encoder at 28–30 Mbps instead of 59, with more loss and freezes.
- **Keep streaming when the network changes.** A device whose address changes mid-stream, such as a phone moving from Wi-Fi to mobile data, can keep its stream instead of reconnecting. The device proves its new address with the stream key first; the host then moves control, video and audio there, sends a keyframe and learns the new path's pacing and bitrate from scratch. Only devices that support it (Nightjar) use it. `client_roaming` (on by default) turns it off.
- **Control messages arrive right after a network outage.** When the network came back after a short outage, what the host had sent in the meantime (an end of session, rumble, HDR changes, link notices) could take another 1 to 3 s to reach the device. The host now resends it as soon as it hears from the device again: 85 ms after a 10 s outage on loopback, against 1.0 to 2.9 s before. Every Moonlight client benefits.
- **Encoder focus** (`encoder_focus`, off by default). `static` gives still parts of the picture, such as a HUD, text or an editor, more of the bitrate than moving ones; `centre` also favours the middle of the screen. The host checks at startup that the encoder honours this for each codec; a codec that fails the check streams exactly as before.
- **Where frames go missing.** The console's stream details show how many frames per second the display produced and the host captured, encoded and sent, and how many repeated an unchanged picture. The 5 s stream timings in the host log carry the same rates, and so does Nightjar desktop's overlay. The log's stream timings also count the device's error correction loss reports.
- **Each launch starts one stream.** A second start with the same launch is refused, an RTSP handshake that stalls is closed after 10 seconds, and a launch nobody connects to expires after 30 seconds.

### HDR and Dolby Vision

- **HDR scene data (HDR10+ and HDR Vivid).** HDR streams carry per-frame HDR10+ brightness data in HEVC and AV1, and HEVC streams also carry HDR Vivid, so a display that tone-maps with them can follow each scene instead of one static peak. The data is measured on the GPU from the captured picture while the frame encodes, about 60 to 90 µs of CPU per frame. Each frame carries the data of the frame before, so a scene cut shows one frame later. Moonlight drops this data from HEVC before its decoder; Nightjar keeps it, and in AV1 it reaches any decoder that reads it. Devices without HDR10+ ignore it. Packed 4:4:4 and PyroWave carry static HDR10, and the host log warns when an HDR stream gets no scene data. `hdr_dynamic_metadata` (on by default) turns it off.
- **Dolby Vision on HEVC and AV1.** HEVC HDR streams offer Dolby Vision profile 8.1, and AV1 HDR streams profile 10.1, to devices that ask for it. Each frame carries a Dolby Vision RPU built from the same brightness data, over the stream's HDR10 picture, so devices without Dolby Vision and stock Moonlight get the HDR10 stream they always did. On a TCL C6K TV, a Dolby Vision AV1 stream carried its RPU on every one of 2317 frames, and the TV's Dolby Vision AV1 decoder took it as Dolby Vision from the first frame. `hdr_dolby_vision` (on by default) turns it off.
- **HLG.** HEVC, AV1 and PyroWave HDR streams offer HLG to devices that ask for it, such as a TV or phone whose screen shows HLG and not HDR10. The picture is graded for a 1000-nit display and the screen tone-maps it from there. HEVC HLG streams carry HDR Vivid data while HDR scene data is on. With HLG on, HEVC also offers Dolby Vision profile 8.4 (HLG with an RPU on every frame). Other devices get HDR10 as before. `hdr_hlg` (on by default) turns it off.
- **10-bit SDR streams never carry HDR10+, HDR Vivid or Dolby Vision data**, whatever else the stream says.
- **Windows HDR Calibration on virtual displays** (`virtual_display_hdr_calibration`, off by default). An HDR stream on a new virtual display gets the Windows HDR Calibration profile of the PC's physical HDR display, unless the device has its own `hdr_profile`, and the virtual display gets back what it had when the stream ends.

### Co-op

- **Split-screen co-op through Nucleus Co-op.** A game Nucleus Co-op has a handler for, and that you added in Nucleus Co-op once, gets a second library entry with "(Co-op)" after its name, next to the game. Set it up from the game's page in the console (**Play together** › **Set up co-op**): it downloads Nucleus Co-op with its progress and opens it on the PC when the game still needs a handler. Launching the entry starts one game instance per player. Each device that launches it next takes the next free slot and streams its own instance, with its controller driving only that player.
- **Each player sees and hears only their own game.** Each stream shows only that player's game window, at the device's stream size, plays only that instance's sound (Windows build 20348 or newer; older builds say so) and sends rumble back to that device. Keyboard, mouse and a second controller never reach the PC's desktop, and the clipboard stays the PC user's. When the game crashes or the session ends, every player's stream ends with the reason.
- **Who can join.** Every paired device that may play games can take a free slot. **Can join co-op games** on a device's page keeps one out; turning it off during a game ends its stream. A device that disconnects keeps its slot for two minutes and gets it back when it launches the entry again. Nightjar's End session and Disconnect work for a co-op player too.
- **Co-op on Home.** Home shows each running co-op game with its players and how long an away device keeps its slot. **Remove** takes a player out, **End co-op** stops every player's stream (both ask first), and **Let … join** lets a paired device take a free slot.
- **Background streaming runs through Nucleus Co-op.** A background stream now runs the game as its own Nucleus Co-op instance and captures that window alone, away from your monitors, so the PC's displays, layout and focus stay with you. There is nothing to choose. A game without a handler, or a PC that cannot run Nucleus Co-op, refuses the background launch and says why instead of streaming your monitors. Background streams take controllers only.
- **Child sessions are gone.** Windows signs a child session in only with a Windows password, and a Microsoft account that uses only Windows Hello has none. Rubylight doesn't ask for one, and doesn't ask you to turn Windows Hello-only sign-in off. On a PC where a development build set child sessions up, the host puts Remote Desktop, its firewall rules and the session settings back as they were and deletes the password that build had saved. If that fails, Help says why and offers Restore again. A saved `background_mode` becomes `nucleus`.
- Rubylight downloads Nucleus Co-op 2.4.2 at the first co-op launch, checks its size and checksum, and installs it into a folder only administrators can change; **Nucleus path** points at your own copy instead. Co-op needs the host to run as the Windows service, on a Windows build that can capture a single window; otherwise the console says why. A co-op launch that cannot run says why on the device: Nucleus Co-op could not be installed or started, no handler for the game, or the session is full or the device may not join.

### Audio

- **PCM, Dolby Digital and Dolby Digital Plus.** A device that asks for them gets uncompressed PCM (stereo up to 7.1.4) or Dolby Digital and Dolby Digital Plus (stereo and 5.1) instead of Opus, for TVs, soundbars and receivers that decode them themselves. Dolby comes in 32 ms frames, so its sound reaches the network up to about 30 ms later than Opus; Opus stays the default on the device. A format this PC can't encode is refused with the reason before the stream starts, never swapped for another. The Windows package includes FFmpeg's AC3 and E-AC3 encoders (LGPL, license in `licenses\ffmpeg`).
- **7.1.4 surround.** A device that asks for 12 channels gets 7.1.4 Opus. The height channels come from a playback device that has them; played on 7.1, 5.1 or stereo speakers, they are mixed into the speakers below.
- **Night mode** (`audio_night_mode`, off by default) evens out loud explosions and quiet dialogue, so a stream can play quietly late at night, with no added buffering.
- **Headphone surround** (`audio_headphone_surround`, off by default). A stereo device gets the game's 7.1 or 5.1 mix rendered for headphones, at the same loudness as the stereo downmix. The stream stays stereo Opus, so every Moonlight client works. Night mode and headphone surround can be set for the host, per device, per app, or for one session.
- **Haptics from game audio** (`audio_haptics`, off by default). A DualSense on the device feels the game's bass and impacts when the game sends no vibration of its own, with strength, response and noise gate settings. It stops the moment the game sends its own haptics or rumble. The audio path gains no latency.
- **No audio gap when the sound stops.** Windows' loopback capture delivers nothing while no app plays, so the host fell quiet for 50 ms before it sent silence, and devices heard an underrun each time a game went quiet. The host now keeps the stream running at its normal pace through silence, at the same bandwidth.

### Clipboard, files and folders

- **Clipboard sync.** Text copied on the device goes on the PC's clipboard, and text copied on the PC goes to the device, as you copy it. It works with Nightjar (with its Clipboard sync setting on) and with Moonlight V+ clients. The device needs "Write to the clipboard" and "Read the clipboard" in its permissions. A copy the permissions refuse is logged and shown on the stream card, never applied in part.
- **Copy images.** Devices with clipboard permission can copy images to and from the PC as PNG. Images are checked before they are decoded (at most 16384 pixels a side, 32 megapixels and `clipboard_image_max_mb`, 16 MiB by default), and a refusal names the limit. `clipboard_image` (on by default) turns it off.
- **Shared folders.** Paired devices can browse folders of the PC, download from them and, with a write grant, upload to them. `shared_folders` names the folders, and **Devices** shares each with a device to read, or to read and write. Files are read and written as the signed-in Windows user, only inside each folder; links and junctions are neither listed nor followed. Uploads follow file transfer's limits. Taking a folder or USB grant away from a device disconnects it. A folder Windows won't let the user read says so, with a hint to check its permissions.

### Console and setup

- **Home, Games and a shorter menu.** Home walks a new user through three steps: pair, see your games, play. The library is now Games, with every launcher in one list: the same tile, switch and status words for Steam and the rest, and one Sync now. The sidebar keeps Home, Games, Devices, Settings and Help; the rest folds under Advanced. When a readiness check fails, Home says "Needs attention" and links to Help.
- **Settings shows what most people change.** Settings holds ten settings in plain words: this PC's name and pairing, the stream preset, the virtual display, whether your monitors turn off, sound, microphone and controllers, closing the game when the last device leaves, and automatic updates, plus wake and sleep, updates, signed-in browsers and the password. Search covers every setting and opens it in All settings, where only expert groups start folded. Defaults say what they do.
- **Help is "Something is wrong? Start here."** It reads the last 8 MiB of the host log and explains the known failures it finds: AMD encoder hangs, codec and start failures, GPU resets, lost capture, ports in use, UPnP, timeouts and network loss, roaming, and a missing or old display driver. Each finding says how often it happened, what to do, and links to the matching part of the troubleshooting guide. Nothing is sent anywhere.
- **A second device can play.** When you pair a device from the console, you choose what it can do: Play games (the default), Watch only or Everything. Before, every device after the first could only watch. The device page says what a device can do in words.
- **Pair with a QR code.** Pairing opens with a QR code ready to scan. Nightjar on Android scans it; Nightjar on Windows and Linux pastes the link. It works once, for three minutes, and Nightjar refuses to pair when the PC's certificate is not the one the code names. The QR code and a one-time PIN no longer cancel each other.
- **Webhooks.** Send signed events to your own URLs when devices pair or are removed, apps start and stop, and streams begin and end, with a Send test button and each hook's last result. A launch that replaces a running game sends the stop event too. See [docs/webhooks.md](../docs/webhooks.md).
- **Wake and sleep.** Settings has a Power section: whether devices can wake this PC, each adapter's wake settings, Sleep, Fast Startup and the sleep countdown. **Apply changes** turns on exactly the listed adapter settings after you confirm. `/serverinfo` reports a physical adapter's MAC even when a device reaches the PC through Hyper-V, WSL, ZeroTier or Tailscale. **Sleep after idle** (off by default, 5 to 1440 minutes) puts the PC to sleep only when nothing used it for that long. After a wake, the PC stays awake for up to 5 minutes while a device is connecting. Setup has an optional Wake-on-LAN step, unticked by default.
- **Every installed game, from every launcher.** Steam and Playnite now add every installed game by default, as the other launchers did. **Game folders** in Add games lists the games in folders such as `D:\Games`, one per subfolder, skipping installers, updaters and anti-cheat services. Epic Games and GOG scans can be turned off. A game a launcher no longer has shows "Not installed", with Remove beside it. GOG games installed with only a registry record are listed too.
- **The library sync never changes an app you made.** Syncs rename, update or remove only the apps they added themselves. Editing a synced game makes it yours, and a game you delete stays deleted, Steam games too. Playnite no longer adds Steam games twice.
- **Radeon presets.** A game's Radeon section has a preset picker (Off, Competitive, Smoothness, Quality, Custom) and plain controls: Anti-Lag, frame rate cap, Chill, sharpening, anisotropic filtering, anti-aliasing and Boost. The controls show only what the GPU reports as supported.
- **Rotate the display.** A device's own virtual display is made in its stream's portrait or landscape shape, and a monitor turns to it and back after the stream. With Match the device, the display follows a device that turns during the stream. A display scale set for one device also applies to a monitor it streams.
- **Client build warnings.** The device list shows the user agent each paired device last sent, and warns when that build has a known problem with this host.
- **Setup on a busy PC.** Setup's driver steps now run at normal priority. On a loaded PC the USB/IP checks took about 11 minutes where they take seconds. A step still running at setup's deadline is reported as still going on in the background.

### Devices and input

- **The keyboard follows text fields.** When a device asks for it and has the Keyboard permission, the host tells it when a text field on the PC gets or loses the focus, with its kind (text, number, password), so Nightjar opens and closes its keyboard by itself, and keeps it closed for a field you closed it on. `keyboard_follows_focus = false` turns it off. Sending the text around the cursor for suggestions is off by default and never covers password fields.
- **Keyboard layout.** Nightjar reports its keyboard layout, and with `always_send_scancodes` the host turns keys into scan codes from that layout, without changing the PC's input languages. `keyboard_layout` picks a layout for a stock Moonlight client, which reports none.
- **Device-drawn cursor.** With `capture = ddx`, a device that asks gets the pointer's shape and draws the pointer itself, and the host leaves it out of the video. `client_cursor = false` turns it off.
- **Touch profiles.** Per device, `touch_mode` turns touches into a mouse or a trackpad, with two-finger scroll and right click (`touch_gestures`) and a sensitivity setting. **Pen barrel roll:** a pen that reports how it is turned in the hand passes that turn to Windows.
- **Host menu.** Hold L3+R3 for a second, press Ctrl+Alt+Backspace, or hold the top-left corner to open a menu drawn into the stream, so any Moonlight client can use it: Back to game, Disconnect (the game keeps running), End session and Close game, with the same permission checks as the console. The game gets no input while the menu is open. `couch_menu = false` turns it off.
- **USB forwarding** (off by default). A USB device plugged into a device, such as a flash drive or a USB gamepad, can be attached to this PC during a stream, over USB/IP inside a TLS tunnel on the stream's HTTPS port; no USB/IP port is open to the network. Turn on **USB forwarding** under General and tick **USB devices** in a device's permissions; **USB devices at once** (1 to 16, default 4) caps how many this PC takes at the same time. The Devices page lists each device's attached USB devices with their state and a Detach button, and a failed detach says why. Nightjar for Windows and Linux forwards devices from its stream controls; on Windows a device needs the WinUSB driver. [Setup and limits](../docs/usb-forwarding.md).
- **Keep monitors off while paused** (off by default). A monitor that wakes or re-announces itself over HDMI while a device is away could switch back on and take the game ([#56](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/56)). With this on, an exclusive layout keeps switching such monitors off until the device returns or the restore delay ends. While it holds, Win+P cannot bring a monitor back; the restore hotkey still can.
- **A confirmed game switch.** Starting another game closes the running one only when the person on the device confirms the prompt. A remembered launch or a retry after a cancelled prompt no longer closes it. The host log says why a game was stopped and which device asked.
- **Controllers.** No more "Controller release failed (0x80070016)" warning at every stream start. Two Steam Deck controllers at once no longer get mixed up. A Nintendo controller on an older gamepad driver plays as a DualSense, keeping its gyro, and the session says to run setup again. A refused controller shows the driver's error. Rumble stops reach the device even with rumble off.

### Fixes

- A device that sends input faster than the PC can take it no longer grows the host's memory without limit: past 512 waiting events the stream stops and says to reconnect.
- Security: a browser form on another site can no longer use saved console credentials. A new console password also revokes the API tokens. A wrong PIN guess from one device no longer spends the PIN for another. Webhooks refuse URLs that resolve to this PC. The pairing, Moonlight and console ports close connections that send nothing or stall, and cap connections per address.
- An update whose "installed" record could not be written is no longer rolled back at the next service start, and the update check no longer holds up launches and the console.
- After a crash, display recovery keeps the restores that failed and tries them again at the next start. On a host whose only display was the virtual one, it no longer fails at every start.
- A second stream on a shared virtual display joins it again after the first stream changed its rate, turned, or Windows changed the size.
- A virtual display that fell back to WGC capture gets the frame ring again once it is recreated, without a host restart.
- A microphone install that fails is tried again later, ending a stream while its microphone is still opening no longer holds up other devices' microphones, and a host that cannot find its own files says so instead of a silent microphone.
- Turning USB forwarding off detaches every forwarded device right away. Several image clipboard requests at once can no longer add up to gigabytes of memory.
- A virtual controller that will not unplug keeps its slot until it does, so the next device's controller is not plugged over it.
- A touch with an out-of-range contact size no longer crashes or misplaces touch input.
- When the host can't check its PCM and Dolby audio at stream start, the device gets the reason instead of a dropped connection.
- Packed 4:4:4 HDR streams take static HDR10 from the control channel and run no scene-data pass; the console says so.
- Running setup again after a failed first install imports Vibepollo's or Apollo's settings instead of refusing. Uninstall says what it could not remove, and a factory reset that leaves files lists them.
- Stopping a stalled display recovery helper on a busy PC no longer fails. File transfer on an administrator host without the Windows desktop says how to fix it.
- After a device roamed twice, a new connection from an address it left connects at once.
- The Wake-on-LAN card says why its first check takes a while (about 25 s on a fresh start), and a console left open on Updates no longer keeps sleep after idle from firing.

## New in 3.0.0-rc.3

- **Fresher frames under VRR.** With variable refresh, Windows can capture each frame of a fast game twice or three times, a fraction of a millisecond to 4 ms apart. The host now times the game's own frames instead of each capture, so it waits for the game's next frame when it is close instead of taking an older copy. A 120 Hz game on a 60 fps VRR stream reaches the encoder with an age p95 of 2.3 ms instead of 3.7 ms (the October 24 measurement), and present to sent p95 is 4.7 ms instead of 6.4, at the same median and the same 60 fps. A 60 Hz game took a stale frame less often too, and a 240 Hz game measured the same; the 1000 Hz virtual display has no frame interval to wait for and works as before. [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md#october-26-vrr-claims-follow-the-games-pictures).
- **Sleep PC (Experimental).** A paired device you allow can put the host PC to sleep while nothing is streaming, through the same `/power-actions/v1` requests as Vibepollo, so a client needs one code path for both hosts. It is off for every device until you tick Sleep PC under the device's permissions in the console; a device imported from Vibepollo keeps its `allow_sleep` grant. The host refuses while a stream, an app or a controller session is active, uses a single-use token that expires after 30 seconds, and suspends only after its answer has been sent and the connection closed. It needs a PC with S1, S2 or S3 sleep, as most desktops have, and is unavailable on a PC with only Modern Standby: Windows has no call to enter Modern Standby, and on such a laptop with hibernation on the request hibernated the PC instead. There the host advertises no Sleep PC, refuses the request, and the console and the host log say why.
- **File transfer (Experimental).** Paired devices can send files to the PC and download them again, through the native API. It is off until you turn on File transfer in the console's Network settings, and each device also needs Upload files or Download files, which no device has until you tick them under its permissions (a device paired before this version, here or in Vibepollo, has them taken back once). Files go to and come from the signed-in user's Downloads\Rubylight, or a folder you choose, written as that user under a `.part` name and renamed only once all of it arrived; a taken name gets the next number. Uploads are limited to 4 GiB by default and leave 2 % of the drive free, at least 1 GiB and at most 4 GiB. While a stream runs, uploads and downloads together move at 50 Mbit/s by default. A device runs one upload and two downloads at a time, the PC eight downloads. A device refused for lack of room can send again at once; before, its next upload could be refused as busy for a moment.
- **Stream in the console (Experimental).** With the WebRTC server on, the console's new Stream page plays a game from the PC in the browser tab: H.264 1080p60, stereo, keyboard and mouse, signed in with the console session instead of pairing, on the local network and Tailscale. [How](../docs/webrtc.md#stream-in-the-console-experimental).
- **Steam Controller input from clients (Experimental).** The host reads the Steam Controller attribute messages a client sends and turns Steam's haptic reports into rumble, as Vibepollo does; a motor whose off never arrives stops after at most 10 seconds. A malformed message is logged once per client, and other input is unaffected. Neither reaches a game yet: that needs a driver with the Steam Controller profile, and nothing new is offered to clients.
- **A lost control connection always ends the stream as lost.** When a client vanished and the host's control worker ran late, ENet's own peer timeout could drop the client before the host's ping check saw it. The host then ended the stream as if it had closed it itself (0x80030023), and the stream could count as a clean fast-rate probe. A client ENet drops after it went quiet for `ping_timeout` now ends the stream as failed, like the ping check.
- **A host started without administrator rights offers PyroWave again, and starts apps with your own environment.** Such a host gave the programs it started (apps, client commands and the PyroWave check) the system's environment instead of yours: the profile folder `C:\Users\Default`, user name SYSTEM, `C:\Windows\Temp` and the system `PATH` only. The AMD driver keeps its shader cache in the profile folder, so the PyroWave check compiled every shader again, took 24 s instead of 1.4 s on an RX 7900 XT, ran past its 15 s limit, and clients were not offered PyroWave. Programs now get the host's own environment. The installed service and a host started as administrator were not affected.
- **The host replies from the address the client reached (#54).** It answered from whichever of its addresses Windows routed by, and a client drops replies from an address it did not connect to. The host now replies on control, video (PyroWave too) and audio from the address the client reached, as Moonlight's own networking does, which addresses the failure to reconnect (error -1) reported with Tailscale next to the LAN. Only a client Windows would answer from another address is pinned; a plain LAN stream sends as before. If Windows refuses to send from that address, as after a VPN disconnects or when its interface has no route back, the host answers that client from its routed address and tries the client's address again after 5 seconds. The host log names the address each stream's client reached, and a refused control connection now says why instead of being dropped silently.
- **A launch that fails to start its app no longer leaves a pending session.** When the app or its settings could not be loaded after the display was prepared, the client got an error but the host kept the launch, its display lease and `RustHostPendingSessionCount` for up to 30 seconds. Such a launch is now released at once.
- **A virtual display Windows moves to another target keeps its stream layout.** On some systems (an RX 9070 XT streaming 4K HDR to a TV, [#56](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/56)) Windows listed the active virtual display under another adapter target and desktop name. The host kept trying to switch on its old, unavailable target every second and could not reapply the exclusive layout, so the physical display stayed on and the desktop kept rearranging. The host now follows the display by its device path, takes its new name, and reapplies the layout and HDR there.

**Tested.** The workspace tests (core 1117, host 779, Windows 512, setup 94, plus the smaller crates), clippy, the web console checks (108 tests) and the release tooling tests (45 and 13) pass on this build. On an RX 7900 XT (Windows 26200):
- **VRR, a 120 Hz game on a 60 fps stream** (HEVC 2560x720, 25 s runs, ABBA against 3.0.0-rc.2, four runs each): age at the encoder p50 / p95 / p99 0.24 / 2.34 / 3.37 ms against 0.25 / 6.61 / 8.06, present to sent 2.90 / 4.71 / 5.69 ms against 3.40 / 8.79 / 10.33, and 0.1 % of frames claimed more than 4 ms after they arrived against 7.7 %, at the same 60 fps. A 240 Hz game measured the same (age p95 0.26 against 0.28 ms, one run each). [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md#october-26-vrr-claims-follow-the-games-pictures).

## New in 3.0.0-rc.2

- **Automatic update checks run on schedule.** The host now counts the time since the last check by the clock, so a PC that slept checks as soon as it wakes, a new check interval applies at once without a restart, and a check that fails because the network is not back yet is first retried within two minutes (backing off after that, up to the check interval) instead of after a whole interval. A game left running no longer holds checks back (only a stream does). Maintenance shows the next check and the cause of a failed one, and the host log records every check.
- **Clients can ask for 10-bit SDR.** A client that negotiates a 10-bit format with `hdrMode=0` (on /launch or /resume) now gets a 10-bit SDR stream: the virtual display stays in SDR and is not prepared a second time, and the encoder uses the 10-bit SDR path (packed 4:4:4 included). No `hdrMode` keeps HDR as before, and so does `hdrMode=1`, including the host option to prefer 10-bit SDR. The log says when it applies. No protocol change.
- **Adaptive pacing from the first frame for Wi-Fi clients.** Clients that report a Wi-Fi or cellular route (Nightjar, `x-rl-video[0].clientWireless:1`) get adaptive packet pacing from the first frame on H.264, HEVC and AV1. It starts at twice the encoder bitrate, speeds up while the link stays clean, and backs off on loss. Clients that do not send it are paced as before. Turn it off with `wireless_adaptive_pacing = false`; H.264, HEVC and AV1 are then still paced with the Wi-Fi route policy from the first frame (also with host adaptive bitrate), and the encoder's keyframe size cap is the smaller one a Wi-Fi host gets. A manual pacing limit still wins. For PyroWave the hint only stops the client's wired-link hint from setting the starting rate.
- **Video packets always have the size the device asks for.** The packet size setting (`packetsize`) replaced it, so a Moonlight that asks for smaller packets on a route it treats as remote (1024 bytes over IPv4, 1184 over IPv6) got 1200-byte packets it could not receive: a green, broken picture from the first frame with no packet loss on a Steam Deck (#52). A smaller setting was unsafe too, because Moonlight rebuilds packets lost on the network at its own size. The setting is gone from the console; a `packetsize` left in the config is ignored with a warning. Reproduced with the harness client asking for 1024 bytes on an RX 7900 XT: `packetsize=1200` failed every video receive on 3.0.0-rc.1; with the fix the stream ran at 1024 bytes and decoded all 676 frames.
- **Simpler settings in the console.** Clearer names and help (for example "Still-picture resend rate"), fewer settings in the basic view, and the capture troubleshooting settings folded away with a count of the ones you changed. The virtual display default now says that it depends on the Windows version, and Activate a virtual display stays visible while it is on.
- **WebRTC server (Experimental).** Off by default (`webrtc_server`). Samsung Tizen TVs and browsers that speak the Moonlight WebRTC TV protocol (version 2) can find the PC, pair with a PIN on the console's PIN page, and stream H.264, HEVC or AV1, with HDR when the display is in HDR, stereo, 5.1 and 7.1 audio, gamepads with rumble and a gamepad mouse mode, and keyboard and mouse. Paired devices show a WebRTC badge on Devices, where you can disable or unpair them. Signalling is plain `ws://` and only local network peers are served. Moonlight and GameStream behave as before. Frame rates from 30 to 120 fps are offered at every mode the AMF throughput probe says the encoder can carry (1080p, 1440p and 4K at 120 fps on an RX 7900 XT), lost packets are resent (NACK and RTX in the default offer), and sending is paced. [Setup and the extensions for client authors](../docs/webrtc.md).
- **Radeon settings that could not be put back are retried on time.** The host retries settings it could not restore once a minute; a console page that read the Radeon state more often than that put the retry off for as long as it stayed open.
- **Packed 4:4:4 is no longer refused when its first frame is slow.** On an RX 7900 XT the packed check's first 1080p frame sometimes took 4.7 seconds to upload and convert before the AMD encoder had it. The host counted that time against the encoder, dropped the frame and refused the stream with "packed encoder failed to return frame 1", which looked like 1080p at 120 fps being unsupported. A frame's two-second limit now starts when the encoder accepts it, in streams too, so a slow conversion no longer drops a frame the encoder has not had time to return. The host log now lists the packed 4:4:4 modes offered to clients at start, warns about each one whose check failed and why, and writes one "packed 4:4:4 stream" line with the packing version, coded size and bit depth when such a stream starts. With full chroma on, /serverinfo now carries `Packed444Modes`, the same mask the host sends in DESCRIBE (1 HEVC 8-bit, 2 HEVC 10-bit, 4 AV1 8-bit, 8 AV1 10-bit), so a client can leave out a mode the host does not offer before it launches.

- **The bitrate no longer collapses on Wi-Fi, and settles sooner on a link below the request.** The controller counted data packets a reordering path had not delivered yet as loss and cut a clean 150 Mbit/s Wi-Fi stream to its floor; it now counts them only while the round trip is up and over what that path reorders. On a 50 Mbit/s link asked for 75 Mbps it cuts within 1.6 s of the first loss and loses 1.1 to 1.2 % of frames after the start-up (6.7 % before), and a probe that fails holds the rate under the failed one for 20 s. A queue that never drops a packet (the RTT 15 ms over its 30 s floor for 2 s after the rate was raised) is cut by 12 % too, and the stream card says "queue". Tested: own measurements on an RX 7900 XT with a shaped NUC client, details in `rust/PERFORMANCE.md`.
- **Nightjar says why HDR or the refresh rate is not what the stream asked for.** Experimental: we have no HDR10 phone and none that switches between several refresh rates. On the Statistics overlay, a screen that stays at another refresh rate than the frame rate vote now shows the likely cause (no such mode, battery saver, heat, the Android refresh rate setting, or a switch that would blank the screen), and HDR asked for but not delivered shows its reason (the screen or its current mode has no HDR10, the PC reports SDR, no mastering data, the decoder's output is not HDR). HDR is requested only when the screen reports HDR10 in its current mode; HLG alone no longer counts. [Details](https://github.com/RamazanKara/nightjar/blob/main/docs/expected-hdr10.md).
- **The host lowers the video bitrate when the network cannot carry it** (`link_rate_adaptation`, on by default). When a device loses frames that error correction cannot repair, for example 150 Mbps asked of a 100 Mbps link, the host cuts the bitrate to what the link delivered, says so in the log and on the stream card ("Bitrate limited by the network: 9 of 15 Mbps"), probes back up in small steps and returns to the requested rate after ten clean seconds. A link that carries the stream sees no change. Off for PyroWave, with Host adaptive bitrate, and once a client sets the bitrate itself. Changing the bitrate never forces a keyframe on H.264, HEVC or AV1; H.264 cannot encode below about 5 Mbps at 1080p60 whatever the target (its quantiser limit), so a cut there stops at that. [Details](../docs/configuration.md#lower-bitrate-when-the-link-cannot-carry-it).
- **A client can be told when the host lowers the bitrate for the network** (new control message `0x5538`, [documented](../docs/control-messages.md#0x5538-link-notice)). A client that asks gets a short notice on each change, at most one a second and never on the video path: the requested and the current bitrate, and whether the host is probing back up or at its lowest. Nightjar says so in the stream overlay ("Host lowered the bitrate to X Mbps: network"). A bitrate that only drifts inside one state is sent at most every 5 seconds, so the line does not flicker. Clients that do not ask hear nothing, and the protocol version is unchanged.
- **A Radeon profile for each game.** In the app editor, **Radeon** picks Competitive (Anti-Lag on), Smoothness (the game's frame rate capped at the stream's), Quality (anisotropic filtering and the driver's anti-aliasing enhancement) or Custom (up to 24 settings you list). The host writes them when the game starts, through the same journaled path as the Radeon controls of the Session API, and puts the original values back when the session ends, also after a crash. A setting the driver does not offer is shown in the session and in the host log; the game still starts.
- **The session says when a launcher is in the way.** If the game does not appear and a Steam, Epic Games, EA app or Ubisoft Connect window wants an update or a sign in, the owner's session carries a `launcher` note (`session.launcher`) that a client can show. The host only reads window titles every 2 s; it never clicks or types into a launcher.
- **Microsoft Store and Xbox PC games in Add games.** The host lists the games of the registered packages that ship a `MicrosoftGame.config`, plus games in `XboxGames` folders, and starts them the way the Start menu does. Games without a package for this Windows user are shown as unavailable with the reason. Console-only titles are left out.
- **A Stream preset control in the device editor** sets the preset for one device.
- **Uninstalling no longer leaves the Intel libvpl license folder of 2.6.x behind** in `C:\Program Files\Rubylight`. Only folders Rubylight itself filled are removed; empty folders of your own, and everything when the install folder is a drive root, stay.
- **The display driver is repaired when it goes missing while the service runs**, not only at service start. The service checks every minute and repairs after the driver has been gone for a minute, never during a stream (a host that cannot say whether it streams counts as busy), never for a device you switched off in Device Manager, and at most once a day per version. A host bound to a LAN `bind_address` cannot say whether it streams, so it is not repaired by itself. To repair it at once run `butterpollo-setup.exe --repair-drivers --now` from the Rubylight folder in an administrator shell: it shows progress and the result and is not held back by the daily limit or `--no-display-driver`.
- **The startup encoder probe no longer raises an "AMF returned no output" warning** when the driver is slow on its first frame; it is logged at info.
- **Lower latency with VRR on a game slower than the display.** With VRR the host used to claim each captured picture on arrival, so a 120 Hz game in a 60 fps stream reached the encoder 4.7 ms old (median). The host now waits for the game's next frame when that frame is at least 2 ms away, as it already did without VRR: measured on an RX 7900 XT, the picture is 0.27 ms old at the encoder and leaves the host 3.5 ms sooner (median), with no change in shown frame rate. A source at the virtual display's 1000 Hz is claimed on arrival as before. [Numbers](PERFORMANCE.md).
- **A lost first keyframe no longer slows the next stream, and a client on a slower port is paced for it from the first packet.** Only loss that lasted is remembered now (the stream's first keyframe lost with nothing else wrong is held for 10 clean seconds, then forgotten), the normal rate is tried every second stream instead of every fifth, and history is kept per host network interface and client link, so another network adapter starts fresh. A client that reports its wired link speed (Nightjar) caps conventional-codec pacing at 4/5 of the slower port, but never below 1.1 times the encoder bitrate. Records saved by earlier releases are ignored. See [Packet pacing](../docs/configuration.md).

When the AMD encoder check finds a codec missing, the host log, a console warning and a refused launch now say why: a setting, the driver's error by name (`AMF_NOT_SUPPORTED (10)` instead of `10`), or that the GPU's video engine has no such encoder (no AV1 before RDNA 3; no encoder on RX 6300 to 6500). Nothing is switched off by GPU name; the check still decides. An AMF driver older than the host's AMF version is told apart and retried with its own version. ## New in 3.0.0-beta.1

- **Three stream presets: Lowest latency, Balanced and Reference quality.** One setting, **Stream preset** under Streaming, fills in the AMD encoder settings that nobody set. ## New in 2.4.1

- **Your display layout is remembered ([#33](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/33)).** Switching Windows to **Show only on 2** (only the stream's display), or making the stream's display the main one, during an extended stream was undone at the next connection. A layout your client asks for, and the exclusive and isolated layouts, are left as they are.

## New in 2.4.0

- **Rubylight's own virtual display driver.** Setup now installs Rubylight's display driver instead of libvirtualdisplay (SudoVDA), and removes libvirtualdisplay from earlier installations once Rubylight's driver runs. The host reads each frame straight from the driver, which saves about 0.9 ms at 240 Hz over Windows Graphics Capture. On PCs with Smart App Control, Windows in S mode or an App Control policy, Windows refuses such a driver: setup and the console say so and what you can do, nothing is left half-installed, and streams use a physical display. Two devices streaming one virtual display with different settings both get a picture: the second captures it with Windows Graphics Capture.

HDR streams of HDR games and displays are unchanged. The peak brightness a virtual display reports stays, as **Settings → HDR → Peak brightness** (`hdr_peak_brightness`, earlier `rtx_hdr_peak_brightness`, which is read under its new name). - **Smoother streams once pacing slows down.** On a fast wired link the host sends each frame itself between pictures. Under congestion, a batch of packets now waits at most 4 ms in total for a full socket buffer instead of 4 ms per packet, which could hold a frame for a quarter of a second without network offload. Reading the stream's statistics, for the console or the 5-second log, no longer holds up the send thread while it sorts them.

## New in 2.3.1

Frame rate and slow-network fixes, measured on an RX 7900 XT host: Warp-style requests now get the frame rate they ask for, and TV boxes with 100 Mbps Ethernet get a picture.

- **A picture on TV boxes with 100 Mbps Ethernet.** Behind a port slower than the host and a switch with a shallow queue, every keyframe sent at the fast wired pacing rate (up to 800 Mbps) overflowed the queue. The client asked for another, which was lost the same way, and the picture froze. Through a 100 Mbps loopback bottleneck with a 16 or 64 KiB queue, 1440p60 AV1 and HEVC streams at 40 and 60 Mbps delivered no frame in 20 seconds, in 2.2.1 too. Now, once a keyframe is lost, the rest of the stream is paced like Wi-Fi (twice the encoder bitrate), and the picture arrived within about a tenth of a second. Devices that never lose a keyframe keep the fast rate. **Settings → Video → Slow down after lost keyframes** (`keyframe_loss_pacing`) turns it off.
- **Streams run at the frame rate the device asks for.** Artemis's Warp frame pacing launches at 60 fps and asks for 120 or 240 in its stream request; the host streamed 60, so the frame rate would not go above 60 whatever Warp setting was chosen. The stream now runs at the requested rate, as in Vibepollo: a virtual display's refresh is raised for it and the frame limiter follows it. The bitrate you set stays the stream's budget, so each frame gets less of it than in Vibepollo at the same setting; raise the bitrate if your network allows. **Use the launch frame rate** (`limit_framerate`), now off by default, keeps the launch rate.
- **The console shows the frame rate the stream is encoded at.** With **Use the launch frame rate** on, a device that launches at 60 fps and asks for 120 in its stream request is streamed at 60 fps, but the stream card showed 120 as the target. It now shows 60 and the rate the device asked for.
- **Send Artemis's Warp streams at the bitrate you set.** With Artemis's Frame pacing set to Warp Drive or Warp 2, the host multiplied the client's bitrate by 2 or 4 but still encoded at the launch frame rate, so a 40 Mbps setting sent about 84 or 168 Mbps. On a TV box with a 100 Mbps Ethernet port, such as the Onn 4K Pro, nearly every frame was lost. The bitrate you set is now the stream's budget whatever the frame pacing. The log's stream lines also show the requested frame rate, the configured and encoder bitrates and the rate actually sent.

Measured with isolated test hosts and the independent moonlight-common-c receiver: a request launched at 60 fps that asks for 120 delivered 120.0 fps on a virtual display (2.3.0: 60.0), and 120 → 240 delivered 240.0 fps; the virtual display's refresh was raised in place and the bitrate stayed at the client's setting. Through a 100 Mbps loopback bottleneck with a 16 or 64 KiB queue, every frame of 1440p60 AV1 and HEVC arrived (2.3.0 and 2.2.1: none). A real Onn 4K Pro and Artemis have not been tested.

Inactive telemetry and stall monitoring park, the service waits for events, and idle display leases check less often while recovery retains its fast retries. Helpers dispatch before the Tokio runtime starts, completed display watchers exit, and host/service log queues hold 8192 lines.
- **Less work before encrypted video leaves the host.** Video AES-GCM uses `ring` with identical ciphertext and tags. Conventional video sends each FEC block as it is built and reuses packet buffers. Packet numbering, encryption nonces and loss recovery remain covered by regression tests.
- **Release GPU resources when their work finishes.** Cursor uploads no longer wait on the CPU; upload resources and captured images remain alive through their GPU fences. Capture and PyroWave owners release cached texture references, copy reuse waits for that texture's readers, and the last stream restores prior process power and GPU scheduling settings. The graphics fallback converts PyroWave 4:4:4 planes in one draw.
- **Cheaper background queries.** Startup skips unsupported AMF 4:4:4 probes on confirmed AMD-only systems. Server information caches hardware/network discovery for ten seconds while keeping session and authorization fields live; mDNS checks addresses every thirty seconds. Streams rebuild effective settings when their revision changes, selected ICC calibration still refreshes, update metadata retains only consumed fields, and update-panel polling pauses in hidden tabs.

The [performance work log](PERFORMANCE_WORK.md#october-12-resource-use-issue-23) records validation and remaining experiments. Pacing waits and the four capture-helper slots retain their existing behavior. Persistent codec-probe caching, a single-pass 4:2:0 graphics fallback and new resource telemetry remain follow-up work; this release does not claim measured end-to-end latency or idle-power gains for them.

## New in 2.2.0

- **Frames arrive just before the phone's screen refreshes.** A Rubylight client can report, about every half second, how early each frame is ready before its display refreshes ([control message 0x5530](../docs/control-messages.md)). The host then times its captures to the client's exact refresh rate, at a phase that gets each frame there with a small margin before the refresh, instead of letting two independent clocks drift: without it a 120 Hz phone waits anywhere from nothing to a whole refresh (8.3 ms) for its screen, and the wait swings from minute to minute. A 60 fps stream to a 120 Hz screen uses two refreshes per frame, and a frame rate that is no whole fraction of the screen's keeps the host's own pacing. Clients that never report, and VRR streams, are paced exactly as before, and after 3 s without a report the host returns to its own pacing. Unit tests check the lock and that pacing without reports is identical to 2.1.0; the host log says `phase lock applied` about every ten seconds while it is active.
- **Error correction follows the network.** Moonlight clients can report each frame they had to repair with error correction or could not rebuild; until now the host only counted those reports. Now it uses them: a repaired frame raises the share of recovery packets at once, a frame that could not be rebuilt puts it straight back to the configured **Error correction** percentage (`fec_percentage`, 20 % by default), and after 5 seconds without either it falls by one point every 2 seconds, down to 5 %. The configured percentage is never exceeded, the client's minimum number of recovery packets per block still applies, and a client that sends no reports keeps the configured percentage for the whole stream. The encoder bitrate is not changed. **Adapt error correction** (`adaptive_fec`, on by default, also per device or app) turns it off, and the stream is then sent exactly as in 2.1.0. The log says `adaptive fec` with the old and new percentage, at most once a second, and the session API shows the current `fec_percent`. The rules are checked by unit tests: no reports, loss, a clean link, turned off, and the limits.
- **HDR metadata for the client's own screen.** A client can now tell the host its screen's peak, frame-average and black luminance ([control message 0x5531](../docs/control-messages.md#0x5531-display-luminance)). In an HDR stream the host then describes that screen in the HDR metadata it sends the client and writes into the video: the peak as mastering display maximum and MaxCLL, the frame-average as MaxFALL and the black level as mastering display minimum, so the client tone-maps for its real panel. An HDR profile selected for the device on the host, or a peak brightness set for the device or app, takes precedence. When the screen changes during a stream, for example on a foldable, the HDR message is sent once more, and only if the values changed; clients that never send 0x5531, such as Moonlight for Xbox, get the single message they get today. The log says `display caps` with the values and what was applied. The game still renders for the host display's peak, which a virtual display gets before the client connects. Checked by unit tests.
- **The host lists the control messages it takes.** The RTSP `DESCRIBE` answer now carries `a=x-rl-control:0x5530,0x5531,0x5532`, so a client sends Rubylight's own control messages only to a host that takes them. [Control messages](../docs/control-messages.md) describes them for client authors.
- **The stream follows the client's screen.** A Rubylight client can ask for another resolution or frame rate during a stream (control message `0x5532`), for example when a foldable phone opens its inner screen, and the host switches without reconnecting. It applies the latest request once the client has stopped asking for 200 ms, at most once a second, recreates the encoder at the new size and sends a keyframe at that size; pacing and the phase lock follow the new rate. The PC's display keeps the mode it got at the start of the stream, and the picture is scaled to the new size between black bars where the shapes differ. A request for the current mode is ignored, an encoder that cannot make the new size keeps the old one, and PyroWave streams keep their mode. `stream_reconfigure` (default on) turns it off for the host, a device or an app; clients that never send the message see no change. [Control messages](../docs/control-messages.md) describes it for client authors. The request handling, coalescing and rate calculation have unit tests.

## New in 2.1.0

- **Your microphone on the PC.** A client that sends its microphone in the format of Apollo's microphone passthrough is now heard on the PC as **Microphone (Steam Streaming Microphone)**, a recording device Steam provides; Sunshine and Vibepollo have no microphone. Rubylight installs Steam's microphone driver the first time a device sends one (Steam must be installed) and puts your default audio devices back. It is on by default (`stream_mic`) and can be turned off per device or app; it uses UDP port 48001, next to the other stream ports. Lost packets are concealed or repaired by Opus, and at most 60 ms are queued, so the microphone does not lag behind. The packet format matches moonlight-common-c byte for byte, checked against its own test vectors, and [Microphone](../docs/microphone.md) describes the protocol for client authors. Checked through the real RTSP and microphone socket path and with a host-side probe on a PC.
- **Choose the install folder, and install or update while you stream.** The setup window has **Change folder...** for a new installation; an installed Rubylight is updated in its own folder. A folder that already holds other files gets a Rubylight folder inside it, network and removable drives are refused, and a folder outside Program Files is locked to SYSTEM and Administrators because the service runs from it. Setup no longer refuses while Rubylight streams: it says the stream will end and games started from Moonlight close, and **Install** goes ahead; **Maintenance > Updates > Install now** does the same from the console, so you can update from another room and connect again. **Install when idle** and automatic updates still wait for an idle host, and a quiet setup still refuses unless it gets `--end-streams`, so scripts never end a stream by accident. On the RX 7900 XT host an update during a stream took 16 seconds from starting setup to the host answering with all its codecs, and settings, paired devices and apps were unchanged. Setup stops and starts the service once per update, so a client reconnecting right after an update stays connected, and a reinstall over an installed Rubylight checks for a stream before it touches the service.
- **PyroWave SDK 1.0.** PyroWave moves to the SDK version upstream froze as 1.0, which brings a fix for an out-of-bounds read in its analysis shader. The stream format is unchanged, so Rubylight Android and Vibepollo 2.0 clients decode it as before. The encoder now asks Vulkan for a high-priority queue, like the copy and conversion queue. On the RX 7900 XT at 1968x2184 HDR 4:4:4 120 fps, picture age was 18.7 ms on average before and after (95th percentile 27.8 and 27.2 ms), PyroWave encoding took 1.05 instead of 1.16 ms, and every frame decoded in all runs (0 decode errors). At 1080p120 SDR it was 14.8 against 14.7 ms. Runs beside a game-like GPU load matched the old build, and the GPU watchdog stayed quiet.
- **Steam covers for newer games.** Newer Steam apps keep their portrait cover under a content-hash folder, and Steam's CDN publishes the large image only under that hash. Rubylight looked for the fixed path, got a 404 and fell back to the small landscape header, which came out cropped. It now finds the portrait cover in the hashed folders and retries the hashed download address.
- **A stray mDNS packet can't stop the host.** The mDNS library used for discovery could index past the end of a malformed record, and with the host built to abort on a panic, one 32-byte multicast packet from any device on the LAN took the whole service down in a local reproduction. Rubylight now uses mdns-sd 0.21.5, which drops such records and keeps announcing; it also keeps its timers on a monotonic clock and expires cached records across system sleep.
- **One product, one download.** Update checks accept the Rubylight asset names (`rubylight-setup-<version>.exe`, `rubylight-<version>-windows-x64.zip`). The portable launcher is now **Start Rubylight.exe**; setup and in-app updates remove the old **Start Butterpollo.exe** and refresh the Start menu entry. Hosts up to 2.0.1 only offer an update that carries a `butterpollo-setup` file, so **2.1.0 is the last release that also publishes that copy**; from the next release only the Rubylight names are published, and a host on 2.1.0 or later finds them. If your host is on 2.0.1 or older, update to 2.1.0 once and updates continue from there.
- **Documentation rewritten.** The README and docs now say what Rubylight does, which hardware it has been tested on (including the owner's Kubernetes pods with several GPU configurations and the secure desktop) and its known issues, with the Wi-Fi results from a real client.

## New in 2.0.1

- **HDR works on Moonlight for Xbox.** At the start of every stream the host told the client about HDR twice: first with placeholder values as soon as the display was picked, then with the display's real ones. Moonlight for Xbox sets the TV's HDMI mode on each such message, so it switched the TV twice in a row, and the picture came out washed out and too bright with no HDR badge on the TV ([#11](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/11)). The host now sends that message once, after the encoder has the display's values, as Sunshine does, and again only if they change. Checked on an Xbox streaming 3840×2160 at 120 Hz in HEVC HDR: HDR now works. The test client can now require an exact number of these messages.
- **AV1 recovery without a keyframe is now on for upgraded hosts too.** 2.0.0 turned it on by default (`amd_ltr_frames` 4), but a host whose settings had been saved by an earlier console could carry the old default, `amd_ltr_frames = 0`, which kept it off. On the first start of 2.0.1, a saved 0 is taken for that old default and removed from `sunshine.conf`, so the new default applies; the log says so. Setting it to 0 again turns the recovery off for good. Found on the test host, where the upgraded settings kept a 0 from October 8 and a real AV1 stream logged no long-term reference recovery. Today's console saves only the settings you change, never defaults.
- **One copy less per frame on the service host.** Windows Graphics Capture runs in a helper in your user session, which hands each frame over in a shared texture; the host copied that into a texture of its own before converting it for the encoder. On AMD the conversion now reads the helper's texture directly, and the helper gets it back once the conversion is done. On the RX 7900 XT host at 1440p120 AV1, picture age fell from 10.07 to 9.84 ms and host time from 2.84 to 2.77 ms on average over three pairs of runs, with every picture complete and decoded; reconnecting, killing the client and a 2-minute stream were clean. `wgc_helper_zero_copy = false` brings the copy back.
- **No more "amd_ltr_frames applies to AV1 only" warning at every start.** The startup encoder check asked H.264 and HEVC for long-term references and warned each time. The warning now appears only when you set `amd_ltr_frames` yourself and stream H.264 or HEVC.

  Both fixes have unit tests.

## New in 2.0.0

Rubylight 2.0.0 is the first stable release, after 30 release candidates. It runs on a Windows PC with a Radeon and imports a Vibepollo or Apollo installation in place, keeping its settings and paired devices ([Features](../docs/features.md) lists everything it does). On the RX 7900 XT test host at the native 1968×2184 HDR 120 fps, the picture reached a loopback client 1.5 to 2.2 ms sooner than with Vibepollo 2.0 at every percentile (HEVC 17.8 against 20.0 ms, AV1 17.4 against 18.9 ms on average, rc.24), and it reached a Radeon 780M laptop over 5 GHz Wi-Fi in 13.5 ms on average (95th percentile 14.3 ms) with AV1, 120 fresh pictures a second; [Performance](../docs/performance.md) has the method and the runs. Changes since rc.30:

- **AV1 recovers from a lost frame without a keyframe.** When a client loses a frame that error correction cannot repair, it asks the host to start over. Until now that was always a keyframe, the largest frame there is, sent just when the link is struggling. Rubylight now keeps four older AV1 frames as long-term references, and when a client that supports it (Moonlight for Windows does) reports the loss, the next frame is predicted from one the client still has. On the RX 7900 XT host at 1440p120 AV1, 80 Mb/s, with an 80 ms outage every 1.5 s, that recovery frame was 15.5 to 24.6 KB instead of a 98 to 127 KB keyframe, and the picture came back 4 to 7 ms sooner (stalls of 92 to 100 ms against 96 to 106 ms) in three sets of runs, with every frame decoded. Without loss the picture and frame sizes were the same. A keyframe still follows a second after such a recovery, in case a decoder could not follow it. HEVC and H.264 keep recovering with keyframes: on a test decoder both froze after this kind of recovery. `amd_ltr_frames` now defaults to 4 and applies to AV1 only; 0 turns it off. Since the release it has also been tried with a real client over Wi-Fi: Moonlight 6.2 on a laptop streamed AV1 1440p120 at 50 Mb/s for 90 s while the host dropped 20 ms of video every 1.5 s. All 59 lost frames were recovered this way, with no keyframe requests after the start and no frozen picture, at 117.7 fps and 2.7 ms host processing.
- **Faster recovery when the host itself is on Wi-Fi.** On a host whose Wi-Fi briefly stops taking packets, as in a user's Legion Go log, Windows refuses the frame the host is sending. The client noticed only a frame later and then asked for a keyframe. Now, when more of a frame is refused than error correction can repair, the host stops sending it and makes the next frame a keyframe at once. With a simulated 80 ms outage every 1.5 s on the host (HEVC 1440p120, 50 Mb/s), the picture came back after 88 ms instead of 98 ms and the client's keyframe requests fell from 17 to 1 per run. Without outages nothing changed. Tested with a simulated outage on the test host; real Wi-Fi hosts, like that user's Legion Go, are where it gets exercised next.
- **Smoother video with a bitrate cap or a wireless host link.** When packets go out at close to the stream bitrate, as with `pacing_max_bitrate_kbps` or on a wireless host, sending one frame took long enough to hold up the next one. Video is now sent on its own thread in that case, and a keyframe request drops the frame still waiting to be sent instead of sending it first. With a 160 Mb/s cap at 120 fps on the host, picture age p95 fell from 19.4 to 16.0 ms and a requested keyframe arrived about 4 ms sooner (p95 61 instead of 65 ms). Wired hosts without a cap keep the previous path, which measured better there; `video_send_thread = false` turns the new one off.
- **Troubleshooting for the AMD video engine timeout.** On the test RX 7900 XT with driver 32.0.31041.1004 the video encoder hung inside the driver four times in October testing: three times under heavy loss recovery at 80 to 160 Mb/s, on unchanged builds too, and once in a plain HEVC stream with an experimental encoder setting that is not in this release. Windows then resets the GPU (LiveKernelEvent 141), and the Radeon may stay in Code 31 until a reboot. [Troubleshooting](../docs/troubleshooting.md#the-picture-freezes-and-the-gpu-resets-during-a-stream) explains what to do and what to report. When a stream freezes, the log now names the stream thread and the step it stopped in, says whether the GPU still answered, and records encoder calls that take 50 ms or more.
- **Smaller fixes:**
  - Ten-bit SDR streams on AMD no longer ask for pre-analysis or the quality rate control modes, which need 8-bit input; they use peak VBR as HDR streams do. After the encoder refused a bitrate change, its peak and buffer limits now follow the bitrate it actually runs at.
  - The host counts the error-correction reports Moonlight clients can send and shows them in the periodic stream timings line in the log, so a support log can show how often a client repaired or lost a frame. Nothing in the stream depends on them, and no real client has been seen sending one yet.

  The ten-bit and bitrate fixes have unit tests and passed the HDR metadata test on the host GPU.
- **Under the hood:** benchmark runs now reject captures that fell back to another capture method or size, and can trace every packet batch. The host can simulate a Wi-Fi outage on its own or the client's side for recovery tests, and the test client can save the received video for a header trace. Reconnect tests no longer fail now and then on a slow test-client retry.

## New in rc.29

- **A keyframe request no longer holds back a moving game.** When a client lost a packet and asked for a keyframe, or for lost reference frames to be invalidated, rc.28 encoded the picture already sent again at once, and since rc.27 that counted toward the stream's frame rate. On a moving picture that sent the old picture twice and pushed the game's next frame back. With a game held at the stream's frame rate, for example by RTSS, frames then went out late and unevenly for about a second after each request. Now the request rides the game's next new frame; a still screen still gets its keyframe at once. Checked on the RX 7900 XT host at 120 fps HDR with 20 requests per 35 s run. Pictures sent twice went from 19 to 0 per run, worst picture age from 22-25 ms to 14-15 ms and p95 from 12-13 ms to 10-11 ms. With the picture changing at 240 Hz, pictures sent twice went from 8-10 to 0. A keyframe on a moving picture now arrives about 3 ms later (9.1 instead of 6.2 ms at 120 fps), because it waits for the new frame. On a still 1080p120 desktop it still arrives in 2.4 ms. Not yet tried over Wi-Fi with real packet loss.
- **The physical monitor stays off when a fullscreen game loses focus.** With the virtual display layout set to exclusive, pressing the Win key or Alt+Tab in an exclusive-fullscreen game made Windows switch the physical monitor back on, and it stayed on for the rest of the stream.   - **Saved 60 Hz modes no longer cap VRR:** a 60 Hz mode saved for a device also limited the game to 60 fps under VRR. The frame limiter now follows the stream rate, and a VRR stream never reuses a display prepared without VRR.
- **No silent downgrades:**
  - Automatic picks the encoder by GPU vendor (AMD → AMF, NVIDIA → NVENC). If it can't start, it tries other hardware encoders and says so on the stream card; it never falls back to software.
  - The console's stream card shows the encoder in use, plus warnings for capture fallback, display modes or frame limits that weren't applied, audio route changes and loss, network limits, and missing input features.
  - The console no longer shows NVENC settings on AMD hosts under Automatic.
- **Install and update safety:**
  - A failed update keeps the previous installation working and keeps its backup for a retry.
  - Manual reinstalls and upgrades use the same rollback as in-app updates.
  - Incomplete packages are rejected before anything changes, and downgrades are refused.
  - Migrating from Vibepollo, Apollo or Sunshine checks the old profile before replacing the old host.
  - Driver setup works with non-ASCII user folders and treats "reboot needed" as success.
  - A setup interrupted earlier can't run driver tasks later.
- **Controllers:**
  - DualShock 4 rumble and lightbar now work on ViGEmBus.
  - Repeated feedback failures show up in the log.
- **Fewer ways for a stream to end:**
  - A PyroWave frame that can't be prepared drops that frame instead of ending the session.
  - A handle leak in the capture threads is fixed.
- **Wi-Fi pacing only where it helps:** 2× pacing now applies only to confirmed wireless routes. Wired hosts behind a Hyper-V switch, VPN or Tailscale keep full-speed pacing again.
- **Playnite plugin installed as you:** the plugin is installed and removed as the signed-in user instead of SYSTEM, so a link in a user folder can't redirect a SYSTEM write or delete.

## New in rc.21

- **Security: the Playnite connection can no longer act as the host.** When the host connected to Playnite's connector pipe, a program posing as Playnite could take on the host's identity, which is SYSTEM when it runs as a service. The host now allows the pipe server only to identify it, not to impersonate it. This issue predates rc.20.
- **PyroWave bitrate guidance from measured picture quality.** 6,048 decoded comparisons across desktop, game, dark and HDR scenes set two levels. At 60 fps the picture breaks down below about 139 Mbps (720p), 187 Mbps (1080p) and 747 Mbps (4K). It is clean from about 277, 399 and 1593 Mbps. The console's stream card shows red below the first level and amber below the second, the log warns, and the guides give the same numbers. At 4K, a clean PyroWave picture needs more than a gigabit link can carry; use HEVC or AV1 there.
- **Crash reports can no longer hang the host.** The host used to write its own crash dump, which Windows documents as able to deadlock; under load a test hung 1 run in 30. A separate reporter process now writes it. If that process can't start, the host still starts and logs a warning.
- **Optional AMF limits for recovery frames.** On Wi-Fi a lost frame triggers a full keyframe, several times a normal frame. A new opt-in frame-size cap cut the largest recovery frames at 4K60 and 40 Mbps from 266 to 90 KB (HEVC) and from 154 to 82 KB (AV1), at a small encode-time cost. The defaults are unchanged. AV1 bitrate changes during a stream now use the right AMF property.
- **Fixes from a review of rc.20:**
  - A DualShock 4 battery level above 100% from a client no longer corrupts the controller report.
  - After a quick change of the stream's display, absolute mouse input can no longer land on the previous display.
  - One client disconnecting no longer releases another client's network acknowledgements before its input is applied.
  - A ViGEm controller keeps its rumble if unplugging it fails.
- **Release checks cover PyroWave.** Every release now has to decode PyroWave end to end at 1080p60, SDR and HDR 4:4:4, with picture, motion, cadence and audio checks. The test tone no longer starves under CPU load, so audio checks measure the host rather than the test machine.

## New in rc.20

- **Controller input no longer waits behind a busy gamepad driver.** Every controller update was a blocking call into the virtual gamepad driver, on the thread that also handles keyboard, mouse and touch. When a game loaded every CPU core, that call could stall for up to 287 ms and everything else waited behind it. Gamepads now run on their own thread, and a mouse move behind a stalled controller waits at most about 2 ms. The input thread also keeps its multimedia priority boost: behind time-critical load, input waited a median of 3.5 ms and now waits 17 µs.
- **Snappier keyboard and mouse.** Moonlight's acknowledgement goes out after its input is applied, saving about 25 µs per event. A burst of keyboard and mouse events reaches Windows in one call: 8 events take 38 µs instead of 180 µs. Setting up input when a session starts no longer blocks it for 3-4 ms.
- **One controller in Steam again: ViGEmBus is back.** Steam on the host often listed Butterpollo's virtual Xbox controller twice. Its bundled controller library races its XInput and GameInput backends, and when it happened, Start+Select could open Game Bar and the Steam keyboard. When ViGEmBus is installed, Automatic now uses it, as Vibepollo does: an Xbox 360 pad, or a DualShock 4 for PlayStation-type controllers. Steam lists it once. Without ViGEmBus, the virtual gamepad driver is used as before. The new `x360` and `ds4` choices pick ViGEm explicitly.
- **PyroWave sends sooner and is verified end to end.** The first protected block of a frame is ready in 0.06 ms instead of 0.49 ms at 1080p120, and in 0.21 ms instead of 1.75 ms at 4K60, with identical bytes on the wire. 1,152 frames decoded with PyroWave's own decoder match between the compute and graphics conversion paths. Loss feedback from the client no longer forces extra frames.
- **PyroWave warns when its bitrate is too low.** At HEVC-like bitrates PyroWave shows only blurred grey blocks. Below one bit per pixel per frame (about 55 Mbps at 720p60, 125 Mbps at 1080p60, 500 Mbps at 4K60), the log and the console's stream card say so, and the setting's description gives the numbers.
- **Steadier streams on Wi-Fi.** Video on wireless and unknown network routes is now paced at twice the stream bitrate instead of bursting at up to 800 Mbps, so bursts don't overrun the access point; audio shares that path. Ethernet is unchanged, and `pacing_max_bitrate_kbps` still overrides it. The log now also reports audio the host loses before sending it.
- **Playnite launches more reliably.** Playnite is found and started in the right Windows session, a missing or partial plugin is repaired, failed library syncs retry promptly, and streams survive Steam and Epic launcher handoffs and Fullscreen mode. Failures now say exactly where the launch stopped. See "Playnite does not launch" in the troubleshooting guide.
- **RX 9000 safety.** SmartAccess Video combined with a forced low-latency mode, a combination that has caused GPU resets, is now prevented. A lost GPU is no longer treated as finished work, which could have reused frames unsafely. The troubleshooting guide has an RX 9000 driver section and a test checklist.
- **AMF settings tell the truth.** The script-free console saved the low-latency switch under a key nothing read; it now saves `amd_lowlatency_mode` and offers the AV1 latency mode. With the default ultra-low latency usage the driver already applies both, so they stay on Driver default. A new `amd_split_frame` setting asks the driver to split frames across two encoders where it ships that off, as the original host did. On an RX 7900 XT the driver never splits, so nothing changes there.
- **Honest capture timing.** The stream log now reports WGC's own delivery delay (about 1 ms on a virtual display), which the old numbers hid.
- **Clearer limits.** AMD encoders produce 4:2:0 only, RX 9000 included; 4:4:4 on AMD means PyroWave. The docs and the error message now say so.
- **A new introduction film.** It explains what Butterpollo is, follows one frame through the Radeon path, and shows the measured comparison with Vibepollo 2.0 beside a game.

## New in rc.19

- **Lower latency when the encoder cannot keep up.** At 4K with a high refresh rate, or on an RX 9070 XT with its single HEVC encoder, one encode can take longer than a frame. The host kept claiming new pictures anyway, and up to eight queued in the encoder, each older when it came out. It now waits while two are in the encoder, enough to keep both encoder instances of a Radeon busy. At 5120x1440 HEVC 240 fps on an RX 7900 XT, whose encoder manages 220 fps there, the time from the game's frame to the packet fell from 42.7 to 11.1 ms, at the same 220 fps. Streams the encoder keeps up with are unchanged.
- **PyroWave converts colour on the Radeon compute queue.** Its conversion was the last one still running on the graphics queue, behind the game: beside a game using the whole GPU, a 1080p HDR 4:4:4 frame took 5.6-5.8 ms instead of 0.47 ms. It now runs on the same high-priority compute queue as the AMF path, at 0.54-0.57 ms beside that game, with identical output. At high bitrates PyroWave also starts sending up to 2 ms sooner, after a slow byte-by-byte check of every frame was made eight times faster.
- **AMF no longer drops frames to stay on its bitrate.** Frame skipping was left to the driver; a skipped frame shows on a VRR client as a held picture. It is now off for H.264 and HEVC, as in Vibepollo.

## New in rc.18

- **Two GPUs: encoding on the Radeon works.** With a Radeon for games and encoding beside an NVIDIA card that drives the monitor, the encoder check at startup ignored the GPU chosen in the settings and tested the monitor's card instead. On automatic it found NVENC there; forced to AMF it offered Moonlight no codecs, and the RTSP handshake failed. The check and the encoder now use the selected GPU, and automatic tries that GPU's own encoder first: AMF on a Radeon. A Radeon PC with an Intel iGPU no longer risks picking Quick Sync either. Desktop Duplication, which can only capture on the GPU the monitor is connected to, now says so and points to WGC capture, which works across GPUs.
- **A new launch film** follows one frame through the Radeon path, shows the same-build compute result and what rc.17 brought players, with a new original score.

## New in rc.17

- **Steam Deck and other controllers with motion sensors get a virtual DualSense, as in Vibepollo.** An Xbox-type controller with a gyro or touchpad, such as the Steam Deck, stayed an Xbox pad: it lost its gyro and touchpad, and Moonlight's quit combo opened Game Bar and Steam's keyboard on the host. With `motion_as_ds4` and `touchpad_as_ds4` both off it stays an Xbox pad. A controller that connected while the stream's display was still being set up also lost its motion sensors, because input was dropped until then; keyboard and controllers now work from the start.
- **The first launch no longer fails when an app's prep commands take a while.** A launch expired 30 s after Moonlight asked for it, even while its prep commands were still running, so Moonlight then found no launch to connect to ("no authorized RTSP launch") although the game was running, and only a retry worked. The host also keeps answering while prep commands run, instead of showing as offline to every client.
- **Lower latency:**
  - With the default install, the host learns of a captured frame the moment the capture helper has it instead of polling for it: about 0.7 ms less from the game's present to the packet.
  - Captured audio is sent as soon as Windows delivers it (event mode, as in the C++ host), and the encoder's frame deadlines are met more precisely.
  - Colour conversion on the compute queue runs in one pass instead of two.
  - Taps and clicks with touch or a pen are released on time; they were 4 ms late on average, and key repeat had up to 8 ms of jitter.
  - On a local network, video and audio are tagged for priority as Moonlight asks (DSCP 40 and 56), as Vibepollo does: Wi-Fi sends them ahead of other traffic. Moonlight does not ask for this over the internet.

  - With Desktop Duplication, mouse-look in a game (a hidden pointer that moves) no longer costs frames.
  - VRR follows a game's uneven frame times more closely.
  - The capture helper now gets its first frame on a still desktop, where it could fall back to Desktop Duplication for the whole session (seen on a Legion Go).
  - The check that keeps a stream's virtual display alive costs 27 µs a second instead of up to 31 ms.
- **One encoder hiccup no longer slows the rest of the session.** Since rc.14 any encoder failure moved colour conversion to the graphics queue, which waits behind the game: 7.2 ms instead of 0.9 ms per frame beside a GPU-bound game. That now happens only after a second failure. An encoder that keeps failing no longer leaks memory, and a recovery that produces no frames ends after 5 s instead of continuing.
- **Several clients on one PC:**
  - A second stream no longer changes the resolution, refresh rate or HDR of a display another stream is using.
  - The saved display layout is restored when the last stream ends, not under a stream still running.
  - The layout is also restored when a remote monitor joined during the stream; the physical monitors had stayed off.
  - Clients sharing the virtual speakers with different speaker setups (a stereo phone and a 5.1 PC) no longer keep losing each other's audio.
  - Quitting an app closes only the game the launching client's store client started, and never a program someone opened on the PC.
- **Displays and HDR:**
  - An HDR client on a display without HDR streams in SDR instead of failing to launch.
  - HDR is switched back on the display as it is connected at the stream's end, also after a driver reset or a TV that reconnected.
  - A TV that was in standby when a stream ended gets its original HDR, mode and colour profile back even when the next stream uses it first.
- **Your speakers come back after a stream.** If they could not be set when a stream ended (a Bluetooth headset turned off, a TV's audio not back yet), every later stream restored Steam Streaming Speakers instead. The host now keeps the original devices and restores them once possible.
- **Settings import from Sunshine, Apollo, Vibeshine and Vibepollo:**
  - It reads files those hosts write and load: sunshine.conf in UTF-16 or the ANSI code page, state files with a byte order mark or left empty, paired devices stored with `""` lists, and device commands in Apollo's format.
  - A profile naming files that no longer exist, or holding large logs, links or oversized files, imports with warnings instead of failing.
  - The imported profile is checked with the host's own loaders, so setup no longer reports success for a profile the host then refuses to start with.
  - When an import fails, setup shows why and restarts the previous host's service, including Sunshine and Vibeshine.
  - A previous host whose installer recorded no install folder is found anyway.
  - One unusable device or app override is skipped with a warning instead of failing every stream of that device or app.
- **Updates:**
  - A host set to a specific `bind_address` is no longer rolled back as "did not start".
  - If the service cannot be stopped, it is started again and the update is reported as failed.
  - An update cut off by a power loss or crash is rolled back at the next start.
  - Only the newest two folders of unfinished updates are kept.
- **Pairing:** a second device asking to pair no longer takes over the PIN of the first, and nobody on the network can keep others from pairing.

- **Games:** a game that restarts itself through Steam (after an update or a settings change) no longer ends the stream, and a browser or store client that a game opens is no longer closed with it.
- **Touch and pen:** held touches and a resting pen are no longer cancelled while the other is in use, Shift held during key repeat is no longer released, pen pressure and buttons are sent as the C++ host sends them, and contacts are lifted when a stream ends.

## New in rc.16

- **Audio without dropouts.** Since rc.14 the host sent silence as soon as Windows had delivered no sound for 10 ms, but Windows delivers sound in chunks of about that size, so ordinary scheduling jitter put short gaps of silence into the stream. Silence now starts only after 50 ms without sound.
- **The uninstaller removes only Butterpollo's files.** Run outside an installation, it could take the folder it ran from (Downloads, for example) for the installation and delete all of it, and a custom install folder lost everything else in it. It now refuses when Butterpollo is not installed, deletes only the files the installation lists, and removes a folder only when nothing else is left in it.
- **Touch, pen and absolute mouse land where you tap on a letterboxed stream.** When the PC's display has another shape than the client (a 16:9 PC on a 4:3 iPad), taps were off by up to a few hundred pixels and the bottom of the screen, taskbar included, could not be reached. Input now skips the black bars as the original host did.
- **Password guessing over the network is limited.** Sign-ins sent with each request (Basic credentials) were not counted, so a device on the network could try passwords at full speed. They now share the login page's limit of 10 attempts a minute per address, this PC is never locked out, and a flood of addresses can no longer block sign-in.
- Two crashes that ended every stream are fixed: a controller whose profile changed after it connected, and one malformed cover-image request.
- **Settings that stopped the host are refused or worked around.** One bad value already in sunshine.conf no longer blocks every later save; a value the file would cut short (an unquoted `#`) or that would swallow the settings after it (an unclosed `[`) is refused with the key named; and a network interface name in place of an address, or a folder as the log file, no longer keeps the host and its console down.

## New in rc.14

- **Streams survive encoder and GPU hiccups.** An encoder error mid-stream ended the session, which Moonlight reports as error -7fffbffb; a game holding the GPU or a driver reset was enough. The host now recreates the encoder, keeps the hardware encoder the stream started with, converts on the graphics queue if the compute path failed, and asks for a keyframe. Only failures that last five seconds end the stream. A wait for GPU work that never finishes no longer freezes the picture for good; it gives up after two seconds and recovers the same way.
- **Fewer frozen pictures on Radeon RX 9000 cards.** The encoder input queue was forced to one frame for every client asking for VRR low latency, which exposes a known RDNA4 driver freeze where video stalls while audio plays. It is now set only on request, as in the original backend. A keyframe requested after a client resets its decoder now carries the AV1 sequence header, so the client can start again, and a frame the encoder loses is followed by a keyframe instead of damaged pictures.
- **GPU safety.** Captured textures could be released while the GPU was still copying or converting them, the kind of fault that hangs a driver; they are now kept until the GPU is done, and stale ones no longer pin gigabytes of video memory. The queues that copy and convert captured frames ran at global realtime priority, ahead of the desktop compositor every captured frame comes from; they now run at high priority, still ahead of a game's normal work (`compute_queue_realtime = true` restores it). An HDR stream on an RX 7900 XT had ended in an AMD driver timeout that took the GPU offline until a reboot; its cause is not proven, and these are the two likeliest contributors.
- **Quitting closes games that a store client started.** The Xbox app, Epic, EA, Ubisoft Connect and Battle.net start games themselves, outside the processes an app launches, so quitting left them running. The host now notes the fullscreen game on the stream's display when it started after the launch, and quitting closes it like the app's own processes. Store clients themselves, the plain desktop and Steam or Playnite games are left alone.
- **Radeon RX 6000 cards:** colour settings a driver rejects no longer cost the encoder, the same kind of rejection as the HEVC fix in rc.12; a bitrate the driver refuses keeps the current one instead of failing every frame.
- **Lower audio and input latency.** Audio goes out as soon as Windows captures it instead of on a fixed 5 ms tick, which added 5 to 20 ms and could insert short silences. Input is handled the moment it arrives instead of after a 1 ms sleep, and a finished video frame is sent without waiting for the encoder's next one.
- **The cursor shows on a PC without a mouse.** Windows hides the pointer when no mouse is connected; while streaming, Mouse Keys are turned on so it appears, as in Sunshine. Reported with Moonlight on macOS.
- **Hosts on Wi-Fi:** connected Wi-Fi adapters run in media streaming mode while streaming, with fewer background scans and the stutter they cause. The desktop compositor is scheduled with multimedia priority.
- **RTSS frame limiter applies again.** Before changing a frame limit or a display, the host starts a helper that undoes it if the host dies. Where the host runs in a job that forbids that helper to leave it (a scheduled task, a launcher), starting it failed and the limiter gave up, so RTSS never got the stream's rate. The helper now starts inside the job in that case.
- **The tray icon shows after a reboot.** Windows keeps Explorer's "taskbar created" message from the elevated host, so an icon the taskbar refused at boot never appeared after sign-in. The host now accepts that message and retries until the icon is in.
- **Firewall check:** the installer allows the host on every network, but a block rule, which Windows leaves when its "allow access" prompt is dismissed, overrides it and makes manual port exceptions look necessary. The host now logs such rules by name at startup, and `--diagnostics` lists them.
- **Input fixes:**
  - Absolute mouse, touch and pen follow the stream's display when it is created, recreated or changes resolution, instead of the display at the first input event.
  - A key or button release that Windows refuses (a UAC prompt, the lock screen) no longer leaves the key repeating, or dead in later sessions.
  - A long press with touch or a tablet right-clicks without left-clicking first, as in Sunshine.
  - Print Screen works; a missing virtual gamepad driver no longer slows other input; unplugging a controller no longer leaves a phantom pad; each virtual pad logs the profile it got.
- **Displays:** when Windows switches a TV on together with the stream display, the host no longer hands Windows the TV's leftover modes while switching it off again, the likely reason Windows refused; if it still refuses, the stream starts with the TV on, as in rc.13. Restoring duplicated displays after a stream falls back to letting Windows choose a mode the group can show when it refuses the original one.
- Releases are built, checked, installed and published by one script, and each release tag gets its own CI run.

The RX 6800 XT's HEVC encoder rejects the input colour-range property, which the host treated as fatal: AMF was dropped and the stream stayed black. Wired 1080p60 at 80 Mbps showed no difference between the default packet pacing and a limit of twice the bitrate. ## New in rc.11

- **Fixes "virtual display did not become active before the deadline"** ([#4](https://github.com/RamazanKara/Rubylight/issues/4)). Windows decides whether a newly connected display joins the desktop, and a layout it saved for the same displays (for example two TVs in duplicate mode) can leave the virtual display connected but switched off. The host waited for Windows and failed the launch after ten seconds; unplugging the other displays was the only workaround. When the virtual display stays off for a second after connecting, the host now switches it on itself beside the current displays, as Vibepollo's display helper does. The other displays keep their modes, positions and clone groups; only if the driver refuses that does Windows choose the modes, which the stream's layout restore undoes. A launch that still fails now says whether the display was connected but kept off.
- **Codecs no longer stay unavailable after Windows starts.** The host checks its encoders once at startup. When it started with Windows, the PyroWave check could run before anyone was signed in (`helper needs a signed-in user`, or `optional codec probe exited before replying (0xc0000142)` while the desktop was still being set up), and on this test PC the AMD encoder itself failed to open (`AMF error 1`), so the host offered no video codec at all until it restarted. If no standard codec is found, the host now probes again once a user is signed in, with growing pauses and at most six times; a PyroWave check that failed only for want of a session is repeated the same way, never during a stream. A probe that fails for any other reason, such as a crashing overlay, is not repeated.

**Display layouts and recovery**

- Restoring a saved layout with duplicated displays (two cloned TVs, for example) no longer fails every time. Clone groups are rebuilt before positions, and displays are found by device instead of by a Windows display name that changes when they rejoin their group.
- A setting that cannot be restored after an interrupted stream no longer stops the host from starting. Recovery attempts every step, logs what failed and continues; before, the same failure repeated on every start.
- A TV or monitor that is off or unplugged when a stream ends keeps its HDR, colour profile and mode entries until it is back, instead of keeping the stream's settings.
- The layout restored after a stream is taken before the virtual display is created, so what Windows changes when it arrives (a TV switched off, another primary display, a retimed display) is undone too.
- A display that disappears during a stream no longer keeps the rest of the layout from being restored.
- Displays that stay on keep their timing and position when the layout changes, such as a retained remote monitor during a restore.
- An expired startup guard on a virtual display no longer makes its recovery fail for the rest of the session.
- A TV that takes a resolution but refuses the refresh rate is put back after a failed launch.
- Disconnecting a remote monitor can no longer hang the host while its display is being recovered.

**Streaming**

- The control and input connection survives network errors such as a Wi-Fi roam, an ICMP reply or an oversized datagram. Before, its worker stopped and every later session had no input or control until the host restarted; it now also restarts itself.
- Controller and motion input get their own channels again (Moonlight asks for 48) instead of sharing one with keep-alive messages.
- Delayed keyframe requests and key releases are no longer discarded as replays behind many motion reports (a 4096-message window instead of 64).
- Moonlight's reference frame invalidation requests are read correctly instead of each becoming a full keyframe.
- A frame too large for Moonlight's packet format at very high bitrates is dropped and followed by a keyframe, instead of ending the session.
- Audio delay no longer grows after a scheduling hiccup: a backlog over 30 ms is trimmed back to two packets.
- PyroWave honours the configured pacing limit (`pacing_max_bitrate_kbps`), which helps a client behind Wi-Fi, and no longer sends a catch-up burst after a send had to wait. Wired clients keep pacing at 95% of the host's Ethernet link.
- The video clock wraps instead of freezing after 13 hours.

**Moonlight compatibility**

- Joining or resuming a running game from Moonlight for Android or iOS works: `/launch` always answers with a game session.
- A wrong PIN no longer blocks pairing for every Moonlight device until the host restarts.
- A device that forgot this PC can pair again; it was refused as a duplicate until removed in the console. Vibepollo profiles that list a device twice now load.
- Quitting a game that is slow to exit no longer stalls other devices' requests.
- Odd client resolutions such as 2556x1179 are rounded down instead of refused.
- A device with the launch permission may resume its own game, as in Vibepollo.
- The app list reports HDR only when a codec has a 10-bit mode.

## New in rc.3

- A stream configured for WGC now falls back to Desktop Duplication if WGC cannot open at startup, matching capture recovery. Logs preserve the Windows error and identify the fallback. This keeps the stream available; it does not provide service-mode WGC or establish equivalent VRR/frame-generation capture.
- **WGC compute copies are now on by default on supported AMD GPUs.** On the RX 7900 XT fixture, mean decoded picture age fell from 14.8 to 11.5 ms at idle and from 52.9 to 34.6 ms under heavy GPU load. Loaded fresh-picture delivery was about 3% lower (51.4 to 50.0 FPS); neither path sustained 60 FPS under that stress. Unsupported sharing falls back to graphics copies. Set `wgc_compute_copy=false` in the configuration, restart the host and reconnect to compare; `gpu_compute_conversion=false` disables all compute copies and conversion. These measurements do not establish the RX 9070 XT result.
- **Capture recovery no longer adds a fixed 150 ms pause.** Each active stream releases its old encoder, frames and filters before capture reopens. Failed reopen attempts retain a bounded retry delay.
- If compute cannot share a captured texture, fallback now copies that same frame. Previously a static desktop could stall while waiting for another update. Desktop Duplication also logs its actual format and dimensions on each open to help diagnose restarts.
- Active capture now keeps the display awake, matching Vibepollo. The request ends with the capture worker and restores the thread's previous power requirements. This addresses capture being starved when Windows turns off an idle display.
- Video packet pacing no longer produces catch-up bursts after a late send. It accounts for wire overhead and caps known local Ethernet routes at 80% of link speed. A wired host still cannot infer a Wi-Fi client's capacity; the reported RX 9070 XT latency difference remains under investigation.

## New in rc.2

- **The mouse pointer no longer disappears.** Desktop Duplication reports the pointer's shape only when it changes, so every capture restart (a mode change, the secure desktop, another client's display arriving) lost the pointer until it changed shape. It is now carried into the new capture.
- **HDR10 metadata in AMF streams.** HEVC and AV1 streams from AMD now carry the mastering display and content light level metadata and signal their colour range, as FFmpeg-based hosts do. Verified in the bitstream for HEVC and AV1, limited and full range.
- **Host errors are no longer silent.** A stream that ends on a host error used to return Moonlight quietly to the app list, as if the stream had been closed on purpose. Moonlight now shows an error with a code, and the host log says why (`session failed`).
- **A packet Windows can't send right away no longer ends the stream.** Video and audio packets are dropped instead, as Vibepollo does, and the video socket gets Vibepollo's 1 MB send buffer.
- **Measured against Vibepollo 2.0.** At 1080p60 HEVC HDR on the same PC with the same encoder settings, the picture reached the client in 13.8 ms against 16.0 ms idle, and in 42.4 ms against 96.4 ms beside a game-like GPU load, with 51 rather than 24 new pictures a second. Decoded HDR colours match the expected values on both hosts.

## Install

- `rubylight-setup-2.1.0.exe` installs or upgrades the host, the `ApolloService` service, the virtual display and gamepad drivers, firewall rules and shortcuts, and can uninstall them. Settings, paired devices, the library and covers are kept.
- For a portable copy, extract `rubylight-2.1.0-windows-x64.zip` and open **Start Rubylight.exe**. The first launch offers to import a Vibepollo or Apollo profile and leaves the original untouched. Install the drivers separately in that case.
- These are unsigned test builds. Keep a copy of your configuration and the previous installer for rollback.

## Lower latency

- On supported AMD GPUs, captured frames are copied and converted on D3D12 compute queues, and AMF encodes from D3D12. This reduces waiting on the game's graphics queue. In the historical full AV1 HDR 1968×2184 120 fps comparison beside a heavy GPU load, the picture arrived 7-9 ms sooner, and 50 rather than 45 new pictures reached the client each second. Idle, it arrived about 0.6 ms sooner; encoding was already at the hardware limit (2.8-3.3 ms). `gpu_compute_conversion` (Settings › Capture) turns this off.
- Current defaults use WGC capture with guarded source-phase pacing, AMF `speed` quality, and a virtual display at twice the stream rate. The earlier Desktop Duplication comparisons remain in PERFORMANCE.md as historical measurements; explicit Desktop Duplication remains available.
- Video FEC uses 21-29% less CPU time than the C++ host, with identical output; encrypted packets avoid extra copies.
